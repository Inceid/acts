##### `pri`
* p. 178-179
	* Every successful act of creation begins with divergence and ends with convergence. Divergence involves gathering and organizing ideas from one's experience and sparks of thought. Convergence involves paring down, prioritizing, and selecting ideas to use for completing a product.
* p. 182-185
	* Three strategies to avoid pitfall of nonterminating divergence. **Strategy 1: Archipelago of Ideas: give yourself stepping stones**
		* Gather a group of ideas, sources, points that form the backbone of an essay, book, or deliverable.
		* Upon reaching a critical mass of source islands, switch to convergence mode and link the islands together
	* Structure of an archipelago note:
		* title: the thing you want to make
		* section for each source with an executive summary underneath of highlighted/bolded key points that you want to use in the specific thing you're making/writing
		* clicking on each source's link leads to *another note* of detailed information & commentary on that source, as well as a link back to the original source.
	* when you're sitting down to make something, pull up an archipelago note of it and use it as the fuel for the first draft
- p. 185
	* the archipelago method separates two activities your brain has the most difficulty in performing together: **selection** of ideas and **sequencing** of those ideas, as selection is divergent (open-ended) and sequencing is convergent (closed-ended).
* p. 186-188
	* **Strategy 2: Hemingway Bridge: use yesterday's momentum today**
		* Only stop writing when the next plot point becomes abundantly clear, building a bridge (& momentum) to the next day
		* generalization: for a few minutes at the end of each work session, write down:
			* ideas for next steps
			* current status
			* details you have in mind that you may forget once you step away
			* your set intention for next work session - problem you want to solve, milestone you want to reach
			* optional: send off your draft, proposal, or other IP for feedback
				* for the next session, you may have their input & suggestions to add to the mix of material you're working with
* p. 188-191
	* **Strategy 3: Dial Down Scope: ship something small & concrete**
		* we necessarily have limited time, money, attention, and support.
		* Postponing our desires to "later" often ends up depriving us of the very experiences we need to grow. The problem isn't a lack of time. It is that we forget that we have control over the *scope* of the project. We can "dial it down" to a more manageable size, and we must if we ever want to see it finished. 
		* Not all parts of a given project are equally important. By dropping or reducing or postponing the least important parts, we can unblock ourselves and move forward even when time is scarce.
		* **But do not get rid of them - store them in your Second Brain.** The rejected scraps from one project might become the perfect missing piece in another.
		* Share your concrete scope-limited work before you're ready!
		* Divergence & convergence form an iterative loop.
* p. 192

* p. 196
	* A good rule of thumb for draft termination: what is the smallest version of this I can produce to get useful feedback from others?
	* If you cannot complete this small version in one sitting, take a few minutes to build a Hemingway Bridge to the next time you can work on it. Add the following:
		* Open questions,
		* Remaining to-dos,
		* New avenues to explore,
		* People to consult.
		* Share with someone who can give you feedback while you're away. Save their comments next time.
	* Upon feeling resistance to continuing this project, dial down the scope. Drop the least important features, postpone hard decisions for later. Find someone to help you with unfamiliar parts. 
	* **Keep notes on what you learn along the way!** (like we're doing now)
