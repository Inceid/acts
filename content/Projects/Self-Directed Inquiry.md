# Definition
**Self-directed inquiry** is the capacity to precisely formulate a goal or problem to solve, gather the resources and schedule the time necessary to achieve this goal, and engage in actions that move one progressively towards this goal in a principled, disciplined manner.

# Mindsets
## Mindset: Re-engineering my Relationship to Formal Educational and Information Structures
Stop thinking that there's a pill for everything. `analog:` stop thinking there's a degree for everything, that there's a degree that will turn you into the person you want to become with the skills that you want to accumulate. Seek them yourself. Train yourself. 

You don't need an MPH - you need to understand what elements of a public health curriculum and the knowledge contained therein are relevant to what you want to accomplish in the world. After the MD, I'm done as fuck with formal programs. Too fucking boring and slow. Too much conformity. Too little actual learning. Degrees mean nothing to me now. I want to know what people actually know. 

## Mindset: Re-engineering my Relationship to Other People's Accomplishments
The default mechanism for interpreting other people's accomplishments often involves some mixture of jealousy, shame, and resentment. "Why can't I do something like that? I wish I was that smart". In the past, entire cultural norms have been based on attempts to limit the influence of envy and jealousy on people's behaviors (cite: Nazar). 

### The Proper Use of Comparison
But it's not enough to simply tell yourself or others not to compare yourself to other people. Comparison as a general problem-solving tool is quite useful, especially when abstracted from operations on people. We have to compare all sorts of things in order to make decisions. Do I want a latte or a cappucino?

Sometimes, comparison when applied to people is also useful. Do I want to study with this friend or this other friend? To answer the question, I consider the extent to which my friends distract me while we're studying. Some of my friends are on task more often than others. I'd rather study with them.

Sometimes comparing yourself to other people is useful even when it's not used immediately for making a decision. "Do I want to be like this orthopedic surgery resident, or this internal medicine resident? What about this larger set of residents?" Considering the fact that different people can embody different kinds of values, comparing yourself to them and comparing them to each other, when done well, can offer you valuable insight about what you care about and should strive for in the long term.

That being said, when people tell you not to compare yourself to someone else, that's usually because they're pointing out an instance of comparison on your part that is counterproductive. That doesn't mean comparison is bad, but that your specific usage of comparison in that situation is bad. So the problem is not that comparison itself is a flawed tool. It's the *erroneous application of comparison* in ways that doesn't help us achieve our goals that is flawed.

## Processes: Re-engineering how I work to accommodate nonlinear, dynamic thinking
My mind naturally thinks in several concurrent directions at once. It insists subconsciously on building distributed systems and constantly shifting its focus of attention. To a lot of people, this might look like ADHD. And maybe I do have ADHD or some subclinical form thereof. But it doesn't matter - this is how my mind works.

# Techniques 
Certain techniques help my mind function in its nonlinear ways while being able to get what it wants done.

### 1. Technique: [[Syngenesis]]
There's always at least a minor activation barrier to starting or restarting work on an ongoing or long-term project. In particular, projects that require a large input of work before any demonstrable effects occur from that work - projects with substantially delayed gratification - are some of the hardest to start and continue work on. We can minimize this barrier by the following: 

### 2. Technique: [[Vernacular Craft]] (the "DIY" approach)
If you're interested in learning about something or how to create something, do it yourself. Re-create it yourself. 

### 3. Technique: Rapid interleaving
Don't work on only one project at once. You know how your mind works - you get bored quickly. Rotate rapidly through projects. 

### 4. Technique: Multiple modalities
Use multiple modalities when journaling, studying, researching, and thinking. Visual, linguistic, spatial, musical, and mathematical intelligences can all be leveraged. Don't stick to one mode of expression and thought. The more, the merrier.

### 5. Technique: Iterative/Sustained metacognition
For every activity you engage in, summon a metacognitive narrator to comment on the activity.

## Processes: From Divergence to Convergence
### 1. Convergence: Stop starting and start finishing
If you want to see any of your ideas come to fruition in the world, you must [develop feedback loops in favor of finishing projects](<Feedback Loops for Projects>).

### 2. Convergence: The first instance is a repetition
The first instance of a convergent set of thoughts is a repetition of the same pattern.

### 3. Convergence: Modularizing, then sprinting for each module
Break down and divide a project into tasks that can be sprinted or complete as fast as possible.  

### 4. Convergence: Ignoring faults and others 
The general form of this statement is to avoid divergent stimuli when unhelpful.
I know that my mind has a tendency to ruminate on the flaws of my surroundings and curriculum. 

### 5. Convergence: Consider yourself your own PI 
Intuitively I'd consider this is on the more advanced side, but a mindset that helps me get my overall project apparatus off the ground is considering myself as my own principal investigator. In other words, I'm running the show at the outset. And as someone in the position of a PI, my apparatus has to be appropriately broad to cover all the areas that I would plausibly want to produce work in as well as sufficiently deep/abstract so as to suggest a common or unifying framework for the whole approach.
