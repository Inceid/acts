## 4.1. Introduction.
### 4.1.1.

In this lecture CMK will argue that valuing one’s humanity entails valuing others’ as well. This response leads to a discussion of the normative status of pain and other animals.

## 4.2. Obligating one another.
### 4.2.1.
We understand from previous lectures that valuing one’s humanity is equivalent to treating one’s humanity as a practical, normative identity. CMK takes for granted that this forms for us a sort of ‘moral identity’ from which we get obligations, the exact form of which depend on our moral citizenry.

But we also need to argue that this can only be the case if there are other citizens which you value as well as yourself! What some philosophers (and Kant himself) mistakenly do is assume that normative self-government arises from private moral considerations.

This is why they believe that the logical step from valuing oneself as a normative agent to valuing others is required.

“Either the individual’s private reasons are served by attention to other people’s reasons … or the individual’s private reasons are found logically to commit her to taking other people’s reasons into account…” This is the standard sort of effort made to bring one’s own morality into the public, social square.

But these arguments are potentially faulty. Prichard notes their risk of being irrelevant, since moral conduct by definition doesn’t mean self-interested conduct. It also falls victim to the free rider problem \[a person may make a consistent exception in his self-interest to not be moral if it doesn’t affect the overall state of his society, and therefore act as a free rider on the benefits provided by others in their acting moral\].

Objections:

(1) The argument from logical consistency, as many neo-Kantians argue, also suffers in that I can recognize that my humanity is normative for me, and that my reasons motivate me to be moral. I can also grant that your humanity is normative for you, and that your reasons motivate you to be moral.

(2) But this doesn’t mean that your humanity is normative for me. Neither does it mean that your reasons motivate me to be moral. Human beings might acknowledge each other’s reasons and identities, but not subsume those reasons and identities under their own sense of normativity.

The above objections both note that private reasons will remain private, and reason cannot make them into public ones. The way to answer them would be to show that all reasons in the sense of products begotten from reflective endorsement are inherently public. The way CMK argues this is by asserting that reasons are inherently shareable between agents considering moral maxims. Morality must appeal to our social nature if it is to be inter-subjectively normative, as described above.

To act on a reason itself is to act on a maxim that is to be shared with others, given its regulatory nature. This will be discussed in the following parts.

### 4.2.2.
Reflection of personal identity obligates me to myself, therefore reflection of your personal identity would obligate me to yourself. And we will show that reflection in itself contains the mechanism for me to recognize your personal identity, as well as why the above argument holds.

### 4.2.3.
Take Wittgenstein’s private language argument for instance, where a private language would only express concepts and experiences that you alone could understand the meaning of, and that was incommunicable to others. Wittgenstein said this could not exist, since the meaning of any words in this language would be relationally normative – they would require a legislator of this meaning and a separate citizen that obeys it. Therefore the idea of a private language is inconsistent with normative meaning.

Note Wittgenstein states that the relation between the legislator and citizen is not merely causal, but also requires the possibility of disobeying the given commands o meaning. The possibility of wrongdoing or misdeed is what gives the words their meaning.

There is a similarity between normativity in language here and normativity in practical reasoning. It takes two to make a reason a reason, since reason is normative and normative is a relational term.

### 4.2.4.
Two points arise here. One, that the same mistake is made between thinking meaning is a mental entity and thinking that reason is a mental entity. We talk about these things not as entities at all, but as relational concepts we share and demand of ourselves and each other.

Now the private language argument doesn’t mean to say that I can’t have a personal language, that is, a language that only I know the details of and keep to myself. But it shows I cannot have a language incommunicable to anyone else. The language in its nature, if it portends to mean anything, must apply to another just as it does to me.

### 4.2.5.
Cooperation that is both compulsive and normative forms obligation, for it combines the causal connection between the commander of cooperation and the submitter with the normative agreement by the submitter to do so without resistance.

### 4.2.6. – 4.2.10.
It is nearly impossible for humans to not intrude into each others’ consciousness, and this tells us strongly that such self-consciousness has some intrinsic publicity to it. This is what informs public discourse – we share reasons because they are intrinsically shareable – we speak a similar normative language, which is why we can obligate one another at all.

And we further know that others’ reasons have similar standing to us as our own. We don’t need reasons to take others’ reasons into account – we typically use their reasons to do so. In fact, we tend to need reasons not to take them into account.

This doesn’t exactly establish that other people’s reasons are reasons for me, but it does say that, psychologically, humans are susceptible to each other’s maxims. This also goes to show that, when we communicate reasons to each other, just like language, we accept each other’s reasons and take them into account. And moreover, by even bothering to respond to those reasons, we give them a legitimacy analogous to that which we give our own reasons. Normativity forces people to obligate one another and treat one another’s reflective reasoning as their own.

The move to moral obligation comes when we demand others to consider how a good or bad action might apply to them if they were in your position – this forces them to consider the option of empathy. There is no other real option but to do this as a human being.

### 4.2.11. – 4.2.12.
So the myth of ethical egoism is that it is not possible to consider a normative moral action to apply to oneself in his human capacity, but not to another self in their own. We have no option but to collectivize normativity.

## 4.3. The origin of value and the value of life.
### 4.3.1.
Pain as a phenomenon can be used to object to a lot of the arguments previously stated. For one thing, pain seems to be a uniquely normative entity, and appears to exist as a real phenomenon in the world. Furthermore, pain is capable of violating Wittgenstein’s private language argument, since it can be a type of mental experience incommunicable to others. It sounds like it would also dethrone humanity as the fundamental source of value.

### 4.3.2.
Now as to answer the Wittgenstein point, he himself issued a form of a generalizable line of argument against such realist-naturalist types of world views – that you cannot ever be wrong, thus your view is unfalsifiable and invalid with regard to possible epistemological claims in the normative realm. If my moral obligations come from punishment from a legislative sovereign and I commit a bad action without being punished, then my action can never be wrong. If my desires inform my obligations, then I can always reference a desire to justify my actions and never be wrong.

In the case of pain, it doesn’t seem that ‘never being wrong’ makes quite as strong of an objection. Pain might well create a fairly stable foundation for morality, where we see sensations as intrinsically normative entities, where ‘never being wrong’ isn’t exactly as big of a problem.

Discussion of above points:

Just as a clarification, I believe the argument as to why one can never be wrong about being in pain follows such:

(a) If I am in pain I would say so.

(b) If I am not in pain I would never say I am in pain.

(c) Therefore, I am always right when I say I am in pain.

(d) Therefore, I am never wrong when reporting the sensation of pain.

### 4.3.3.
But we can be wrong about whether we are having such sensations! The above argument supposes that people only ever use language expressively – that is, every time somebody says ‘Ouch! I’m in pain!’, that they are simply crying out in actual pain. But this is not true! Perceiving / sensing pain is fundamentally different to using language to report it.

And we are never referencing actual sensations when we’re evaluating them as to whether they are “right or not”. We are only referencing propositions about those sensations after they have taken place. So, not only does the statement ‘I can never be wrong about whether I am in pain’ not make any sense, but we might not even be able to judge it as right or wrong in the first place, since it isn’t necessarily a proposition that regards the physical sensation of pain directly!

### 4.3.4.
One might argue that pain does form a sort of mental sensation in addition to its physical symptoms, which is fair enough. But the thing that makes pain what it is doesn’t come from these sensations, at least not from them in themselves. We take pain to be painful because we are inclined to fight these sensations. We take pain to be painful because there is a conscious entity that can ascribe things like painfulness to sensations.

Without us, nothing actually counts as painful. So it would seem that pain is no intrinsically normative sensation at all! Since it derives its normative repugnance from our conscious rejection of it.

Now one might ask why we fight against it if it is not bad in itself. And in some sense, we are biologically designed to fight against pain. But this is only as a way of taking care of ourselves, not a normative reason to do so. An evolutionary interpretation itself would endorse this view – pain is not an overridingly horrible thing for a biological organism, but simply one of many mechanisms of finding out what is physiologically wrong with one’s body at the time.

Furthermore:

“If the painfulness of pain rested in the character of the sensations rather than in our tendency to revolt against them, our belief that physical pain has something in common in grief, rage and disappointment would be inexplicable. For that matter, what physical pains have in common with each other would be inexplicable, for the sensations are of many different kinds. What do nausea, migraine, menstrual cramps, pinpricks, and pinches have in common, that makes them all pains? (Don’t say they’re all horrible; that’s just repeating yourself.) What emotional pains have in common with physical ones is that in these cases too we are in the grip of an overwhelming urge to do battle, not now against our sensations, but against the world… pain is not the condition that is a reason to change your condition, the condition in which the natural and the normative are one. It is your perception that you have a reason to change your condition.”

This brings intrinsic normative value right back to the reflective consciousness, since it is the faculty that perceives things like pain.

### 4.3.5.
Furthermore, if we were overwhelmingly moved to eliminate pain, as the previously objected-to view articulated, then we would not be compassionate to anyone else’s pain! Why take on someone else’s misfortune and thereby increase our emotional burden rather than leave them be? It is through our mental capacities that we recognize and think of acting to change the misfortune of others for the better.

### 4.3.6.
Pain is but a perception of a reason to change our condition, as previously argued, since:

(a) pain is a sensation.

(b) conscious organisms preserve their identity through responding to sensations.

(c) therefore pain is essential to a conscious organism’s identity.

(d) therefore pain consists of a perception of some reason to act to change one’s condition.

### 4.3.7.
Now note that an agent’s pain is an unreflective objection to a threat to his identity, whereas obligation is a reflective objection. Otherwise they are fairly similar in nature.

### 4.3.8.
One might object in the same sense as to the voluntarist position regarding the ‘slippery authority’ argument: if I do not experience regret or remorse by not acting on obligations, can I ignore my mind’s commands to myself?

Now, the mind’s authority over itself also entails an ability to experience negative moral emotions – that is, to sense pain or remorse upon not acting on its obligations. If you don’t feel pain or remorse, your mind didn’t have authority over yourself in the first place. The two are necessarily connected. So, if your mind had proper authority over your actions, then you will not ignore its commands and obligations.

### 4.3.9.
As a side-note, these kinds of claims can work on highly evolved conscious animals other than just humans. In these cases as well, life forms an intrinsically valuable thing, and still only in the sense of reflective endorsement, not as a normative entity in the world.

### 4.3.10.
From here, the argument moves in this general direction: my human identity, and yours, depends significantly on our animal identity, in the sense that we value ourselves as animals with similar significance as we do as humans in a required sense in order to value anything at all. Then this means we must recognize and endorse our animal nature, and, through the intrinsically public nature of reasoning, endorse other animals’ normative natures as well.

So we do have moral obligations to animals as well as humans.

### 4.3.11.
With regards to pain, remember that pain can also threaten multiple identities at once – the physical identity, in that it retards our ability to think clearly and react to phenomena since we are focused on the pain, for one instance. So it may be difficult at times to, in the first-person sense, consciously perceive the reasons pain imposes on us to act. And in another sense, while we might say animals feel pain, we cannot say they reflect on it in the same way as we do, since in the vast majority of cases they are not self-conscious as human beings are.

But note also that pain shares a reflective structure with reasons. An animal in pain not only rejects its painful condition, but also rejects in being in that condition that gives rise to its painful condition in the first place. This is not trivial, as it imbues animals with some recursive sense of awareness of their situation.

And this is why many might pose the argument for pain’s intrinsic badness, since we (humans and animals) almost always object to it when we experience it. The key, again, is almost always. This isn’t enough to say pain is intrinsically bad.

Now note that value exists in the deliberative perspective, but that doesn’t mean it’s always in the mind and not in the world. We might turn away from pain because it is painful to us and because the pain depicts some evil in the world. The reason it is evil, though, is because of the value we ascribe to it.

### 4.3.12.
CMK argues along this line that we do not have significant obligations to plants, since they cannot be conscious and thus cannot have a self-identity that is shareable with us. Nevertheless, the fact that they are alive might well move us to respect them as well, in the same way as we are alive and share the identity of “aliveness” with all other living beings.

So, insofar as a plant is a relatively complex form of life, we might well respect it as one. Disrespect of such an entity would reflect badly on our own character.

Now this is an insufficient account of our obligations to animals, but it works for less complex forms of life such as plants.

### 4.3.13. – 4.3.14.
There is another argument to be made that animals have their own obligations we can expect from them, though weaker than ours since they are more susceptible to impulsive thinking (this comes as a consequence of a lesser-developed conscious self).

Now much of this might be attributed to simple domination of humans over animals, rather than moral obligation. In a sense, this discussion has a point – we do have an animalistic competitive nature as humans, and we use this nature to pit identities against one another. Nietzsche and Freud happen to think that morality and this fundamental nature, along with human consciousness itself, arose at the same time. This would complement CMK’s own view. However, she also holds that human consciousness uniquely arose as an antithesis to the domineering, savage animalistic nature of humans, which disciplined itself into self-government.

This is also consistent with Nietzsche’s own view. When an animal’s practical identity becomes complex enough, it can impose standards of behavior upon itself, most significantly by self-imposed punishment – guilt, regret, and sadness at not living up to a specific aspect of one’s standards is what brings about normative self-government.

Both Nietzsche and Freud also made the general case that normative self-government has its limits. Upon expansion of this self-consciousness to a large-enough degree, it will become self-destructive, hating itself and its own animal nature, and destroy all value along with it eventually. This begets a nihilist value system of the kind both thinkers warned.

Nietzsche hopes for the kind of “turning against oneself” that the animal nature of humanity had in moral development, but this time for human self-consciousness. The result would be the superman, who would be exactly to us what we are to the animals.

The reason why we brought up this discussion in the first place is to respond to the idea that obligation has its limits, or perhaps that too much obligation is bad. Furthermore, it is worth considering that CMK’s own view is relatively consistent with their more genealogical explanation for moral obligation.

## 4.4. Skepticism and Suicide.
### 4.4.1. – 4.4.2.

Now note that this account of obligation in general bridges the fact-value distinction in an interesting way. When we look at a sensation or normative concept from outside the reflective standpoint, we aren’t looking at a value. We are looking at the fact of a value – for instance, take the example:

I am experiencing intense pain, and this pain takes the form of a sensation inside my reflective standpoint (if we take CMK’s earlier arguments and definitions of pain).

Another human can recognize and respond to my pain. This is because my pain takes the form of a perception of a reason, and reasons are shareable between persons. Therefore my pain is in a sense shareable with another human.

But this human also conceives my pain within his reflective standpoint.

Note that values can only arise and exist within the reflective standpoint. If we leave the perspective of both of these humans (the one feeling pain and the one recognizing the other’s pain), and describe these sensations, we are talking about facts of values, not values. I can factually say that the person is in pain. Thus we do possess facts about people having normative reflective tendencies, and this creates the fact of normative value. Sounds odd, but it’s true.

Now note that a rejection of this fact of value is in a sense a rejection of all of life and its value, since this fact of value of pain, like others, is a fact of life itself. Such a rejection would be like suicide, and a rejection in this direct form is frequently a motivator for suicide itself.

From CMK’s standpoint, rejection of all value, or a suicide that might result from such a rejection, is profoundly immoral, since it denies aliveness, the condition from which value, and therefore morality itself, arises. But from her standpoint, it’s also true that we cannot really say someone who commits suicide has done wrong – he has violated no value of his reflective consciousness by doing so, since he has no values left.

And yet we as humans still look at suicide with betrayal, disappointment, remorse, just as we do at a person with an utter lack of moral consciousness – we feel that we have also been deeply violated by his deeds.

And this makes the standpoint of normative skepticism, the view that there are no such normative reasons to act on one’s desires at all, dangerous and impossible. For it destroys a reflective consciousness and its morality, as well as ignoring the obvious fact of the existence of reflective consciousness within humans.

## 4.5. Conclusion.
### 4.5.1. – 4.5.5.
To conclude, we bring up the four central philosophical responses to the normative question discussed in lectures 1 – 4.

In a sense, the voluntarist account of a legislator is correct, but this legislator turns out to be the reflective consciousness. The reflective consciousness is the source of all value, in that it creates normative entities by imposing values onto everyday sensations. Reflection has the power to compel us to obey it and to punish ourselves when we do not. Insofar as we can use reflection to do this, we have autonomy over ourselves. And since the reasons reflection provides for normative behavior are in their nature public and shareable, we impose them contractually on other people and animals. So, morality really is normative and binds conscious beings in obligation to each other.
