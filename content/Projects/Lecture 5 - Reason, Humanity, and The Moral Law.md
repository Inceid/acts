> G. A. Cohen’s commentary on CMK’s theses.

I.

I make a law for myself. But does the law bind me, since I made it and must be consistent with my will, or does it not bind me, since I made it and thus have authority over it?

Both arguments assume the same premise and are contradictory, so one must be incorrect. Each of them shows up (and is endorsed) in Hobbes’ Leviathan.

To elucidate, let’s bring up an argument from Leviathan for an all-powerful sovereign.

  

Premise 1. Every act of the sovereign is an act of all citizens. Since each citizen endows the sovereign with representative authority.

Premise 2. It is absurd for anyone to reject what he himself does. Thus it is absurd for one to reject to any law that he himself passes. This law must be obeyed.

  

Hobbes also reasons that the sovereign is not subject to the law itself, since he makes it into a law. According to him, this does not contradict the earlier statement, since the sovereign must be free from control from his own laws in order to be a sovereign. Otherwise he is but a citizen and has only that much authority as a citizen.

  

A further argument:

“The sovereign makes the law… [and] can unmake the law … [thus] is not bound by the law that he makes.”[[6]](https://d.docs.live.net/fe5d9419c2945bf4/Desktop/F2-3-8-18/philosophy/notes/sourcesOfNormativity.docx#_ftn6)

But the citizen, though indirectly by representation through the sovereign, also made the law and is bound to it! What conceptually makes the sovereign so unique that he isn’t bound by it?

Well, he has a point that a sovereign that can make a law and easily unmake it has less of an obligation to obey it than a citizen.

But does a law still not bind the sovereign even before it is unmade?

II.

This question applies to CMK, since she holds a view similar to the first – people make laws for themselves to individually obey.

Her response to the threat of the second argument’s contradiction is to appeal to practical identity, which is itself the construct of the person making the law. For Kant himself, the authority of the person over himself is contingent upon universal laws not coming only from his will, but from something else that ultimately causes the will to act in the way it does. There is a prime mover different from the person himself, and Kant thus buys into the second argument from Hobbes.

The key thing to note is that Kant’s person cannot unmake his own law, since he is moved to make it by his nature. But he can make his law according to this nature, and thus reigns autonomously over it in a sense. Kant also grounds the ultimate legitimacy of moral claims in reason itself, which arises from human nature but itself is a necessary requirement for morality. Human nature is far too contingent to be reliable, he says, and thus we should appeal to reason in its own being as the fundamental root of morality.

This is where CMK differs, for she grounds morality exclusively in human nature, and that she supports the P & H argument that no ‘ought’ arises without laws, and no laws without a lawgiver.

To repeat, the relevant difference is that the reflective consciousness has ultimate authority over its own laws for CMK, whereas it has equivocal authority over its own laws for Kant, since the determinism of human nature also impacts the lawmaking process and exerts its own influence.

So now we ask – who the hell am I to issue myself laws? Why should I obey them? Why should I care? Kant can answer by saying that, while I legislate the law, its content comes from reason, not reflection, human consciousness, human reason, or anything as such, but pure reason. And thus reflective endorsement is in a sense deterministic. But even if CMK were to argue that practical identity is 100% deterministic (which she does not appear to do), this would still not prove any connection between practical identity and morality, given the relative instability of practical identity due to its humanization.

Note that rationality is different from the reflective consciousness, and Kant grounds morality exclusively in rationality. CMK does not.

  

III.

The bigger problem is that the ‘practical identity’ that CMK refers to as providing moral reasons is itself unstable as such a source. For practical identity deals with just that, identification, practicality, loyalty, emotion, and not nearly as much to do with law itself. Practical identity itself is very vague – no concrete limitations are given as to what constitutes one in CMK’s system. And it definitely does not involve as much adherence to principle as CMK says.

I might identify with my desires and reflections on account of my identity, but it might not follow that I thus identify with some principle which that identity itself follows.

If we take R. M. Hare’s terminology, CMK has a somewhat imperative and prescriptivist solution, but not universally so. If it is true (as Cohen is arguing) that in myriad cases, one’s practical identity does not necessarily supply universal legality to his normative reasoning, then some of the normative language and demands I use on other people might just be that – demands, without the force of law.

Furthermore, the consequences of not obeying one’s practical identity are grossly exaggerated by CMK – I wouldn’t lose all of my value nor lose my identity if I never volunteered for a charity; I just wouldn’t feel that good about not doing so. I can do many wrong things and not have them challenge my identity that much at all.

IV.

CMK presents two formulations of the normative question:

First form of the normative question. “Why should I be moral? That is, why should I care about moral obligations?”

Three conditions are required of the solution: that it addresses the normative agent directly, is transparent, and appeals to the agent’s sense-of-self powerfully.

With regards to CMK’s solution, there are issues with how she satisfies all three conditions.

V.

Now her answer in a nutshell is that “the reason why ethical standards make claims on me is that they represent commands that I give to myself, either in virtue of my practical identity.” (SON 178).

A huge issue is present immediately based on her second formulation.

Second formulation of the normative question. “Why should I be moral? \[standpoint of a person protesting the demands of morality on him\]”, and “Why should I be moral? \[standpoint of person self-justifying the demands of morality on him\]”. CMK’s answer addresses the latter person fine, but does no better than her rivals at addressing the former person.

For we can apply the same objections to her own theory of practical identity that she applies to that of her critics.

For instance, she criticizes the moral realists in their response to the second person in that they appeal to some intrinsic normative fact of the world to answer the normative question, and therefore disregard the substance behind the question in the first place. Telling someone “this is your duty, and that’s a fact” might not motivate him to change his mind! He’s questioning whether these obligations really do exist in the first place.

But how can anyone give an answer to a person this skeptical of moral claims? CMK might persuade him to consider practical identity, but if he does not feel the force of obligation, then he shouldn’t feel the force of practical identity either! As her own objection goes, confident assertions of the existence of obligation doesn’t answer the question. Indeed, no assertions can help at this point – a radically skeptical person in this predicament must convince himself of an argument – nothing we can say will do anything.

If we scale down the difficulty of the question, though, CMK’s solution is fine. If I am asked to justify my morals in the face of your radical skepticism, I can do so using her theory. But if I am asked to justify your morals in the face of your radical skepticism, I cannot.

I can’t convince the intransigent to be moral any more than CMK can. But I can convince the sincere questioner why I should be moral, and thereby convince him to himself be moral if the audience is proper and open-minded. We have to consider who our audience is when answering these questions.    

VI.

To note an earlier objection, not all obligations threaten as dramatic damage to one’s identity as CMK would have us believe.

And a more salient objection: people who behave highly immorally can still have impressive levels of reflective endorsement, or at least just as much reflective endorsement going on in their heads as anyone else. So where’s the moral part of the obligation?

CMK answers that the content of the obligations we experience depend on the particular nature of practical identities that different people have. But if this is the case, we have failed the basic requirement that the answer address why we have moral obligations, not obligations in general.

VII.

CMK does attempt to answer the question specific to moral obligation, most substantively in 3.4.7. – 3.4.10. and in 3.6.1.

From those sections, we interpret the following argument.

CMK’s Argument from above cited sections.

1. Since we are reflective beings, we must act for reasons.

But    2. If we did not have a normative conception of our identities, we could have no reasons for actions.

So     3. We must have a normative conception of our identities (and our factual need for a normative identity is part of our normative identity).

So     4. We must endorse ourselves as valuable.

So     5. We must treat (all) human beings as valuable.

So     6. We find human beings to be valuable.

So     7. Human beings are valuable.

So     8. Moral obligation is established: it is founded in the nature of
human agency.


Focus area: 1 – 3

1 is fine.

2 isn’t true, since I don’t need to consult an independent conception of my identity to endorse or reject a specific claim. Merely acting on reasons doesn’t carry any such a requirement. I can have such self-conceptions when making the decisions, but they are not independent in any sense.

3 is therefore not true.

Focus area: 3 – 5
There is no reason for me to think that human impulses are intrinsically valuable, or that all humans and their reflective conscious identities must be treated as valuable in addition to my own.

Focus area: 5 – 7
\[I have no idea what Cohen is saying, and he doesn’t bring up any substantive objections besides a possible fallacy of equivocation. Let’s just ignore this section.\]

Focus area: 7 – 8
An immoral person, such as a Mafioso, can accept 7 and coherently reject 8. The Mafioso may value humans insofar as their identity or actions agrees with his practical identity, but the Mafioso ethic might be very different to morality in general. Again, this addresses an aforementioned issue with CMK’s view – it justifies obligation, not moral obligation.

VIII.
A reference to Harry Frankfurt’s concept of free personhood helps highlight CMK’s audience-difficulty in implementing her solution.

“For Frankfurt, I am free when my will conforms to a higher order volition, when, that is, I act on a first-order volition that I wish to act on, when the spring of my action is one that I want to be moved by” (SON 187). Another restriction we add is that the lower-order volition submits to the higher-order volition, lest we get second-order adaptive preference formation.

But this conception, even with the added restriction, is consistent with the Mafioso ethic. I can defend my ethic to the Mafioso using the above conception as well as CMK’s, but so can the Mafioso with his ethic if I ask him to change it!

And another point that this conception brings up – CMK’s legislative consciousness behaves too much like a Hobbesian sovereign, as opposed to the Hobbesian citizen. Kantian reflective endorsement is in an important sense inescapable, but Hobbesian-Frankfurtian-Korsgaardian-etc. reflective endorsement can of course be escaped by the most antecedent element in the moral process itself: the moral agent.

So appealing to human nature to justify objective moral obligations doesn’t work, and where it does, the original Kantian solution (by Kant, that is), which references a transcendent source of such obligations, does the job better.
