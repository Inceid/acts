# The slip-box method 
Luhmann had two slip-boxes: bibliographical and generative. 
* **Bibliographical**: the format for notes in this box: bibliographic/citation on one side, summary of relevant content on the other side. `meta:` This is analogous to [Captures](<Capture - Capture Criteria>), but on paper and from exclusively written media.
- **Generative**: after looking through the Bibliographical box, he would think about their relevance for his writing and write ideas, comments, and thoughts on new index cards, only one card per idea and using only one side, with an eye towards already existing notes. 
	- Notes were numbered from 1 onwards, with no distinction by topic. Notes were explicitly linked by subsequent numberings: to write a note that refers to note 22, name it 23. If 23 exists, name it 22a. If 22a exists, name it 22b, or 22c, and so on. Then to branch from 22a, make 22a1. So on so forth. 
	- Notes were also linked by name to any other notes that were relevant under the same topic. 
	- An index would list a few notes that would serve as an entry point into a given line of thought or topic, resembling a topic-sorted collection of links to notes.
	- A big feature of the Generative box is connectivity. "Rarely would a note stay in isolation." (19)

# How to write a paper, step by step 
## Generative Mode 
1. Make fleeting notes and put them in your inbox to process later.
	`meta:` these are our Dailies and Perennials. Dailies are our natural background streams; Perennials are special notes on topics we feel strongly that we will come back to later. 
2. Make reading notes to summarize and interpret what you read. 
3. Make permanent notes by going through 1. and 2. and think of summative ideas relevant to [your own research](<Kernel/अन्वेषण.md>). Write them as if you were writing a brief for someone else, with sources and concision.
## Exploration Mode 
4. Start to navigate the system for ideas and questions to write about from within the slip-box.
5. Expand any notes with what is missing from further reading as you explore.

## Consolidation 
6. Once you have selected a topic, gather all relevant notes together, looking for what's missing and redundant. Resolve these gaps.
7. Translate your notes into a rough draft. Isolate and fill holes in your argument.
8. Edit and proofread.

"Most people follow different lines of thought at the same time. They might focus for a while on one idea, but then leave it alone for another while until they see how to proceed further. It is helpful then to be able to *pick up on another idea now and go back to the earlier thought later.*"
