## 3.1. Introduction.

### 3.1.1.
The past series of lectures comprise an evolution from voluntarism to realism to reflective endorsement. We understand that voluntarism threatens an infinite regress with regard to the normative legitimacy of the legislative sovereign, that realism threatens a refusal to answer the normative question, and that reflective endorsement theorists only tackle the problem with regard to general rules, not particular (and potentially unaccounted for) actions.

This forces a move to Kant’s own conception, which allows for self-government in practical action as well. Like the realist, Kant thinks actions have a real goodness or badness in them, but like the reflective endorsement theorist, he uses the test of reflective endorsement to supply this goodness and badness directly from the mechanisms of the human mind.

### 3.1.2.
Two general points will be argued next. First, autonomy is the source of obligation. Second, we have moral obligations.

In lecture 4, natural objections will be considered.

The specific content of the obligations argued for is not considered. We’re concerned with their source as of now.


## 3.2. The Problem.
### 3.2.1.

The mind is self-conscious, not through any biological mechanism or fact of neurology, but through the plain fact that it is reflective upon itself. Humans think about thoughts themselves and form conceptual frameworks about them. Humans are conscious of their own thoughts in the first place. This is the reason why they think about them.

It follows that we can evaluate our thoughts. The capacity to think extensively about X entails a capacity to question X, to scrutinize it critically. We can take thoughts, detach them from our immediate selves, and abstractly pull them apart.

This is when the normative question arises. Now that I can consciously criticize my thoughts, do I still believe in them? I think of a reason Y to believe in X. Is Y a good reason to really believe in X, then? How do I judge Y? I can’t just settle for desires, emotions and impulses as my motivators. As long as I reflect, as long as I criticize myself, I need a reason to act. That is what the reflective mind demands of itself. That’s what the normative questioner implicitly demands of himself. A clear statement of the problem contains a statement toward the answer.

The solution might well come through reflection, the source of the problem itself. The idea is that we find sound reasons to act through criticizing our standards, our actions. The skeptic in this sense, would argue that reflective problems are insoluble and unanswerable. Since the skeptic himself could be the normative questioner, it is pertinent that we answer a criticism like this.

### 3.2.2.

“We cannot conceive of a reason which consciously responds to a bidding from the outside with respect to its judgments, for then the subject would attribute the determination of its power of judgment not to reason but to an impulse. Reason must regard itself as the author of its principles…”

To endorse moral behavior, you have to freely endorse it. Your mind’s own moral compass acts antecedently to any other moral being or recommendation, and you must make it your own law to act on whatever commandments you wish before they become your morality.
- (Comment) That would imply, then, that to accept morality without reasoning that it is an acceptable one is not morality at all, but action by impulse.

Thus, a presupposition of freedom is required for moral claims to have any weight on us.

There is the hard determinism of the Modern Scientific World View that threatens this aspect – after all, human actions at a fundamental level are not free but determined and determinable in all states.

CMK’s Response: even if this were true, who exactly should care? One might be able to predict my actions, but I must still decide them in the face of uncertainty. And suppose I discovered then, that all of my actions were perfectly predictable. Does it follow that I would act differently? Had I discovered that I was to do X, would I do nothing but sit in a chair and wait? Then I wouldn’t do X after all, negating the prediction.

The fundamental problem here is that consciousness distorts determinism into freedom. If I find that I will definitely do X by some mechanism, then I might take precautions against X, perhaps rebel against the mechanism by which I do it, or something else. Either way, it doesn’t actually matter that I was told I would do X. I would still assert my will in spite of this realization. I can find a way to be free in spite of determinism.

To restate, if the realization of determinism diminishes our ability (or even motivation to act), then we might say freedom is defeated. But there is no reason to think determinism necessarily does this! Understanding determinism might make us rebel to be free even more. Determinism offers no threat to free beings, whatever ontological facts it might refute about freedom itself.

CMK answers the above worries (comments) by citing and replying to the determinism that shows we weren’t free in our actions after all, from a third-person standpoint.

The response: why exactly does this matter? Scientists might be able to predict the product of all of my microstates, but they are not the ones choosing to act. I am, and I am doing so within a deliberative perspective, that is, within a perspective that forces me to consider my will and my desires in interaction for determining how to act. Freedom might not be ‘real’ in the scientific and third-person sense, but neither is an agent’s action.

Just because freedom cannot be scientifically explained does not mean anything about its reality to rational agents making moral deliberations. Freedom, rationality, reasoning, all of these come from practical considerations of our position in the state of nature. Scientific considerations come after this.

CMK admits that freedom may be inexplicable in the scientific sense. But this doesn’t matter; freedom is explicable in the sense that it’s supposed to be: from the standpoint of reflective consciousness.

Ultimately, it is better to hold the practical view of reflective consciousness antecedently to the scientific world view, as both provide us useful tools for analyzing the world, but the former comes before the latter in immediate physical, logical and emotional importance.

### 3.2.3.
Under this ideal, ‘reason’ means reflective success. Successful reflective endorsement is required for one to act on a desire or principle. The question is how one decides to actively endorse or reject a moral claim. Is the desire or its object intrinsically normative? Or does this come from something else? A familiar infinite regress threatens here, as well as the realist–anti-realist dilemma.

The answer according to Kant is that the will inherits its normativity from the Categorical Imperative, which the mind uses to decide on acting on a principle or desire. The Categorical Imperative takes the form of a universal law when we use it to test our moral claims, and it is through this that we allow free will to conform to its own laws.

This preserves the will. If the will were following an external law, it would lose its freedom. But if it were to follow no law at all, it would not will things consistently or freely, but by random impulse and thoughtless action. By imposing rules upon oneself, an actor preserves his freedom and acts normatively.

### 3.2.4.
CMK makes an additional distinction not made by Kant himself: the law of acting only on maxims that one can will to be universal laws is the ‘Categorical Imperative’. This is to be distinguished from the ‘moral law’, which is the law of the Kingdom of Ends, where we act only all maxims that all rational beings would theoretically agree to act on in a cooperative system.

This and the argument in 3.2.3. establishes that the Categorical Imperative is the law of free will, but not that the moral law is. The moral law directly applies to all rational agents, where the Categorical Imperative only applies to the agent himself in some consideration.

This has led many to call the Categorical Imperative an empty formalism, along with some formulations of the moral law. The second claim is false; the first one we cannot answer true or false. The second is clearly false because the demands of rational beings (non-domination, cooperation, contract-building, etc.) can be concretely explained to a large extent.

But note that this argument is missing something – it does explain why the Categorical Imperative should bind us, but not why the moral law should bind us. To solve this problem, the agent under consideration should think himself a citizen of the Kingdom of Ends, an at once hypothetical, asymptotic, and real-world societal construct of what a moral crowd would look and behave like.

## 3.3. The Solution.
### 3.3.1.
This proceeding argument cross-references against an argument Kant makes himself in the Critique of Pure Reason.

The rationalists would say that the mind can transparently see itself-in-itself because of its ability to reflect. Reflective endorsement theorists, and Kant, instead say that the mind can produce a conception of itself. But this doesn’t prove the existence of a metaphysical self at all. The self that I am comfortable identifying as might be a total illusion with regard to whatever my true self is, and I cannot simply say that I make up my true self, since self-identification changes rapidly as one ages and progresses through life.

So we’re stuck with not knowing ourselves any better than we know our world.

But with regard to the deliberative moral process, my will chooses the law that I identify with the most as my moral law. How I as an agent identify with the desire / law that I have chosen determines what kind of moral citizen I am – if I truly believe I have no standards for my behavior, then I will act as if I have no standards for my behavior. There will be a sort of congruence between my beliefs and my actions.

This isn’t theoretical. Self-value and self-identification are tremendously important when considering questions of ‘ought’ behavior.

To reiterate, self-conception and self-respect gives rise to obligations. If I respect my will and my deliberative process and its ability to make sense of my jumbled thoughts, then I can be sufficiently obligated by them. This is also what gives rise to unconditional obligations – actions that we would rather die than perform can only be such if performing them destroys our self-conception, self-respect, or deliberative processes. And so we reject these actions as unconditionally immoral.

### 3.3.2.
Given the above argument, this would mean an obligation is just a logical extension of an identity. That is to say, obligation is the reaction of an agent against a phenomenon that would strip his identity.

But it is also true that some identities act antecedently to others. For instance, my identity as a decent human being might act antecedently to my identity as a citizen of X country. And in moments where these two identities conflict either with each other, or perhaps conflict with one’s identity as a citizen of the Kingdom of Ends, there are certain limits to which we can bound certain obligations.

Obligations are always unconditional, but violating them is fairly trivial unless they are ‘deep obligations’, that is, that taking exceptions to these kinds of obligations would engender serious harm on the order of self-identity and the moral law in general.

Exceptions are, then, of course allowed, until we get to a serious threat.

### 3.3.3.
Now the question of exactly how the agent conceives his own identity is still not settled. But we do at least nominally establish that an agent must autonomously command himself to accept a moral idea in order for it to be normative for him, and it is this very act of self-government that makes it a good thing for him.

### 3.3.4.
Now one might mention the fact that, by conforming to actual real-world normative moral laws / standards of conduct, one wishes away his autonomy. But this is not necessarily the case! We’re talking about two different kinds of freedom at this point – the freedom to accept or reject general, long-term claims from reflective endorsement, and the freedom to act privately in one’s own capacity in any particular situation.

One can autonomously restrict their private free will by accepting, for example, taxation via reflective endorsement. One has willed an authority to take control over some of their practical identity, and therefore this rule complements their freedom instead of violating it. Another example – insofar as I am a student, I act autonomously in choosing which teachers or departments to trust my future schedules with, but insofar as I am a student, I am not free to act on my own private reasons for taking courses if required rules from the corresponding teacher(s) and department(s) demand that I not do so.

Autonomy is all about self-commanding, and also depends on the kind of person you are. And it is coherent to say one can autonomously limit his immediate free will by subscribing to a set of general restrictions if he judges that it improves his self-identity and / or reflective capacities in the future.

### 3.3.5.
But how can we know content-wise what might be a good idea to make a universal law? Kant appeals to the form of these ideas / maxims as opposed to their matter to answer this question, and does so in a manner similar to Aristotle:

Matter is the actual materialistic content of an object, form being its functional arrangement in space-time. Goodness might not arise in the parts of a maxim, but in the specific combination of those parts. The functional arrangement of the parts of a statement therefore play a key part in establishing the goodness or badness of a maxim.

The general argument to this point seems:

(1) A good maxim is good in virtue of its internal, functional structure[[5]](https://d.docs.live.net/fe5d9419c2945bf4/Desktop/F2-3-8-18/philosophy/notes/sourcesOfNormativity.docx#_ftn5).

(2) A good maxim can be willed to be a universal law for the agent who is concerned with the maxim through reflective endorsement.

(3) A universal law is what our agent applies to his own conduct in both general and particular situations.

(4) The moral law takes this universal law and applies it to all rational beings.

(5) Therefore anyone capable of conducting a test of reflective endorsement within themselves has reason to abide by this model of normativity.

(6) If our normative questioner can buy into reflective endorsement as providing normative adequacy for his maxims, then we have answered the normative question.

### 3.3.6.
This means, in some sense, that realism is true – there are, in a sense, intrinsically normative entities, though not out there in the world. Instead, they reside as the products of successful reflective endorsement.

Now note it is traditionally (and still) thought that if we can explain why a thing has value, then it does not have intrinsic value. Thus we cannot use critical faculties to figure out intrinsic value. It would then seem that we must know an intrinsically valuable object by intuition. To be specific here, according to this view we cannot give reasons as to why justice is good, because then justice has no intrinsic value. This view is incorrect.

Two sets of distinctions may help elucidate this concern – the distinction between final and instrumental goods, and the distinction between extrinsic and intrinsic properties. The first concerns whether we value something for its own sake or whether we value something for using it to aid in some other end. The second concerns the source of an object’s value – that is, whether the object gets its value from itself, or from some other object.

The intrinsically normative entity that the realist seeks must satisfy both of these properties. Additionally, if we can show that a thing’s intrinsic properties make it into a final good, we need not know it to be so by intuition. For if we prove a thing to be its own source of value, and that the kind of value of this thing makes it into something valuable to us for its own sake, then it is something we must necessarily value.

Again, the correction to realism must be reiterated – values are not discovered in the external world, but are found as products of reflective endorsement. So, they are indeed created by human beings. But they are all created as sources of normative conduct! They all demand to impose some restriction on behavior. So, the realism being endorsed here is primarily procedural moral realism rather than substantive moral realism, the procedure being reflective endorsement.

### 3.3.7.
This forms the first part of CMK’s argument. To sum up, this shows that obligation exists. Furthermore, reflective endorsement is required to solve the problem as to which possible obligations we must act on. It also gives us our sense of self-identity, and actively connects this identity with the maxims we consider. So, by accepting a maxim, we personally identify with and endorse it, and therefore will this maxim to be a law that would regulate our conduct. Thus it becomes an obligation.

But there’s still some relativism involved – different laws would exist for different people, even if the procedure is the same. To establish the particular ways in which we consider our identities as moral thinkers, we proceed to the next step.

## 3.4. Moral Obligation.
### 3.4.1.
We shall change our stance slightly to approach this relativistic problem. To borrow a concept from Rawls’ A Theory of Justice, we need to distinguish between a concept and a conception.

A concept of justice refers to a problem of justice. A conception of justice is a set of principles supposed that makes up the solution to the problem.

The normative force of a conception, then, comes through a self-identification with the concept. Since this is what makes the principle normative for you.

### 3.4.2.
So we need to get from concepts to conceptions. The antecedently present conception of the self’s practical identity is what mediates this transformation. A practical-self-conception embodies both the problem and the solution – we take up the standpoint of a legislative citizen of the Kingdom of Ends, and ask what kind of laws this citizen should adopt. These are the laws we’re inclined to accept. This practical identity is then what leads to the conception of normative action.

### 3.4.3.
Now if this holds, then Williams is wrong to say reflection is not inherent in ‘thick’ concepts. Thick concepts are to thin ones what conceptions are to concepts. They form the normative mechanism by which we judge abstract ones like ‘righteousness’ or ‘justice’.

Furthermore, thin concepts form essential parts of any moral investigation, since they embody the central ‘ought’ problems of moral philosophy. Any other race of intelligent animals then must have also come across the normative question in its development.

But these values do not come to be shared as external, worldly concepts. They are shared inter-subjectively, bound to their rational subjects and perpetuated through endorsement by those very actors.

### 3.4.4.
This alone does not eliminate the problem of relativism. The specific content of moral rightness and wrongness might still vary strongly between geographical and cultural crowds, and we might not have standards to distinguish whether one is better than the other after all.

### 3.4.5.
But this sort of relativism has limits – we can still rank values, using Williams’ conception, by their ability to improve human flourishing. In this sense, we can come up with general ways to assess values to a certain extent.

### 3.4.6.
And note additionally that no argument can preserve relativism on one level without eradicating it on another. A moral relativist has decided it to be the case, objectively (and even perhaps universally), that morality must be relative.

Note that any particular sort of practical identity acts functionally only after reflective endorsement. Furthermore, it is reflective endorsement that informs practical identity, not the other way around.

People who claim that individual identity is utterly empty or that communitarian / socialistic identity prevails over any individualistic conception of the self make this fundamental mistake. For they must use the normative self to accept or reject one of these practical identities in the first place!

### 3.4.7.
But we might well agree that, while normative identity decides practical identity, one’s practical identity must be the source of his reasons to act. Since we accept or reject maxims based on whether we identify with them significantly enough. Still though, most of our self-conceptions are heavily contingent or subject to change between different people, situations or circumstances. Since they are so contingent, they can be shed away. That is to say, when the particular demands imposed by this identity go away, so does the identity itself.

But this is to be strongly distinguished from normative self-government, which is never contingent. This is a central aspect of one’s self-identity – if it ceases to exist, then a person will literally have no reason to do one action rather than another.

And normative self-government does not come from any particular practical identity, but from one’s own humanity! As humans, we require of ourselves reasons to live and to do things. As intelligent animals, we reflect on these reasons and consciously endorse or reject them. It follows that valuing oneself as a human being, which is a given in the vast majority of cases, entails valuing oneself as a moral agent.

This also entails that we value other people’s humanity as well. Objections to this previous point will be considered next lecture, where this point will be more extensively argued.

If this is true, our human identity acts antecedently to any of our other practical identities, since it is required to properly subsume any of them under our own guise. And thus our moral obligations are inescapable.

### 3.4.8.
The above comprises an elucidation of Kant’s formula of humanity as an end-in-itself, which basically means that, since humans are the ultimate source of normative moral value, they are valuable for their own sakes. And to reiterate, they are the ultimate source of normative moral value because they create normativity by endorsing or rejecting moral claims before acting on them. This ideal is implicit in every choice a human makes.

### 3.4.9.
To review, CMK argued that human consciousness…
(a) has a reflective structure that creates normative problems.
(b) requires of itself reasons for action that answers these problems.
(c) creates practical self-conceptions with which it identifies and uses to assess moral claims.
(d) uses these conceptions and their judgments to regulate its conduct.

If we take (a)-(d) together, we understand that human beings actively create moral value and imbue it into the world. Thus, they are the ultimate source of moral value and must value themselves if they wish to act morally at all.

### 3.4.10.
CMK incidentally identifies her argument as a transcendental argument, that is, an argument whereby we take something from experience as granted and then deduce what must be the case for this something to be logically possible.

A pertinent objection might be that values are not real in the same sense, again, that colors are not real, but perspective-dependent and human-created. And the answer will be the same; it doesn’t matter whether they are human-created for its purposes, or if they are not needed to scientifically explain the world. Moral concepts govern our conduct as human beings, and so they are real and objective in their own sphere.

## 3.5. Morality, Personal Relationships, and Conflict.
### 3.5.1.
Given this argument, any reflective agent can be led to acknowledge their moral obligations. We have no choice but to value ourselves as human, and this gives us our ability to act morally.

This doesn’t mean all obligations are moral, or that moral obligations trump all other types of obligations. We might have some incidental identities that we take to be more pertinent at the time, and nobody is a moral agent and nothing more significant.

If morality demanded this of us (see Williams make this argument in Persons, Character, and Morality and Moral Luck), it would be incoherent.

But this doesn’t mean morality is conditional. It, like everything else in life, has its sphere. Conflicting obligations can both be unconditional – life just sucks and is that way.

Two kinds of conflict exist that might threaten this sort of issue, the second one more relevant than the first. The first is that one may have a practical identity itself contradictory to human self-value, which is ruled out by reflective endorsement (hopefully). The second is that a practical identity conflicts with human self-value in particular or contingent cases.

### 3.5.2.
Personal relationships exhibit this kind of conflict. We could interpret autonomy to the extent that it excludes relationships between individuals entirely, but CMK rejects this view.

To properly articulate this view, let’s compare it to another one:

A personal relationship is an emotional tie in which one generally desires to be around another person and cause their happiness / goodness.

This entails that…

(a) even an egoist can have such relationships,

(b) these relationships, unlike moral ones, are governed by affections, not reasons or normative frameworks, and

(c), there is a tension between relationships and morality, in that friendship differs from moral respect, in that motivation by affection is unlike motivation from duty, and in that personal relationships draw us to favoritism and bias whereas morality apparently doesn’t.

This view is articulated a lot by philosophers throughout history, and is bullshit in CMK’s view. For one, the distinction between reason and affection in motivation is incoherent – affection is a subset of what might successfully pass the test of reflective endorsement, which makes up reason.

Furthermore, the account leaves out the willed commitment of each benefactor of this relationship. Personal relationships bolster one’s own practical (and thus moral) identity and commitments, since they involve reciprocal commitments and dealings between human persons.

The fundamental structures, virtues and obligations that regard personal relationships and moral relationships are the same, it’s just the proximity, specificity, and intensity of these elements that would inevitably differ.

And so there’s no obvious reason why one should prefer their obligations to humanity rather than to a person that they have an important relationship with. Thus some intractable problems come up in morality when regarding personal relationships (though there are some degrees of personal bias that would be rendered instable positions by reflective endorsement, both as a matter of fact and to the agent himself at some point).

## 3.6. Conclusion.
### 3.6.1.
Two general points were established, the arguments for them accessible above (literally too detailed to break down into a paragraph or too). “First, the reflective … human consciousness gives us authority over ourselves… it makes [our] laws normative… practical conceptions of our identity [inform the specific content of our self-governing laws]… this leads to the second point. The course of reflection to which this kind of question gives rise leads us to recognize the form of identity which stands behind the others: our identity as human… [normative] identity, [then], exerts a kind of governing role over the other kinds [of identity].”

We now leave three worries unaddressed:
(1) I might value my humanity, but why should I value others’? [egoist]
(2) Why should moral concern be limited to humans? What about other animals? [animal rights]
(3) Why value anything at all? [skeptic]

All are answered in lecture 4.
