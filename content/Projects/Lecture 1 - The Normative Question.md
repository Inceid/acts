## 1.1. Introduction.
### 1.1.1.

Many people tend to think that, if morality had no basis in a lawgiver external to humans, it would not be an objective / real morality, but a mere invention by humans.

However, showing that X is a human invention does not show that X is not objective or not real. Moral standards exist in the only way any standard, objective or not, can exist – people have to first believe them in order to practice them.

This implies two further points – that morality cannot be separated as a framework external to human activity / invention, but that it can at the same suggest overriding principles to govern that activity / invention.

In this way, morality finds a different metric of validation than empirical science – whereas science has phenomena to validate its hypotheses, morality must ultimately have human behavior to validate its own.

For a further distinction between ethics and science, we should understand that ethics, in its study of morality, does not describe human behaviors or dealings. Morality makes claims and demands on current behavior, and the objective validity of these claims rests on whether there is any notable force behind them. This is what we must examine.

## 1.2. The problem.

### 1.2.1.

A moral philosopher wants to do three things: clarify moral concepts, delineate their scope and applications, and explain their source(s). This comprises the bulk of philosophers’ sets of moral concepts.

These sets have direct practical applications. Indeed, take the example of personhood at the subjective conception. People seem to treat each other as persons only when they share important (perhaps axiomatic) virtuous tendencies. Otherwise, people may be ostracized from our society.

We use moral concepts to talk about fundamental values and fundamental decisions people make every day. Moral concepts are used in ways that are deeply profound and important in practical life, in short.

Moral theories provide explanatory adequacy as well as normative adequacy for these observable effects that moral concepts have on practical human behavior. That is, moral theories can explain this behavior soundly, and help us justify or condemn (whichever appropriate) this behavior, thereby making moral demands on us in the present. The claims of morality are pervasive enough that they require justification.

The moral skeptic believes there are no proper justificatory stances we can offer here, and this is the most substantive point of contention between moral skepticism and moral philosophy.

We should note that a moral theory can have explanatory adequacy and still lack normative adequacy, since the former concerns morality from a third-person, theoretical point of view about the nature of morality, whereas the latter concerns a first-person, practical point of view that each and every moral agent must ask themselves when applying such a theory to their situation. The first-person question forms the central normative question of the book.

## 1.2.2.

Any answer we provide to this question must satisfy three requirements. Firstly, it must successfully address the agent we want to convince to be moral. That is, we should convince ourselves in the first-person sense. Moral authority cannot stem alone from addressing / excluding agents who question morality itself in the third person.

Secondly, this theory must be transparent. That is, it must allow an arbitrary agent to fully understand the motives behind his actions and the reasons behind valuing what the theory values. Nothing of the theory or its underlying structure should be concealed from the agent.

Thirdly, this theory must move its agent in a profound way. That is, the agent should adhere to the theory strongly enough that he may sacrifice his life while doing so.

## 1.2.3.

A brief overview of the four major answers to the normative question throughout the history of modern moral philosophy:

1. Voluntarism – normativity derives from a legitimate authority external to that of moral actors themselves.
2. Realism – normativity derives from validation of its intrinsic existence in the real world.
3. Reflective Endorsement – normativity derives from its practical usefulness to us as humans after we consciously endorse its claims.
4. Appeal to Autonomy – normativity derives from an agent’s own will and free action.

Korsgaard (CMK) will present her view as an updated version of 3 & 4.

## 1.2.4.

The rest of this book will proceed with the implicit assumption that some unified theory of normativity in all philosophically significant subjects is possible.

CMK does not explicitly present nor argue for such a unified account, but assumes one is possible given her approach to the normative question.

1.3. Voluntarism.

1.3.1.

Pufendorf and Hobbes (P & H) posit that a Godlike sovereign is required to impose moral values onto the world. Grotius held that moral values that impose their own obligations onto us exist, but Pufendorf counters with the notion that moral obligation is only a human affection brought about by conformation to a law.

Since a law must be imposed by a sovereign, moral necessity requires imposition by a sovereign. Hobbes agrees.

  

1.3.2.

P & H, however, also believe that the content of this imposed morality is determinable by reason external to that of the sovereign. This marks an important distinction between P & H and divine command theorists in general. Legislators only give legislative legitimacy, not the actual law.

P & H also agree that the content of things like morality and justice generally include what is conducive to social harmony.

  

1.3.3.

Additionally, the mechanisms used by the sovereign to impose this law give morality a sense of requirement. This requirement provides a strong incentive in itself for people to observe morality (note that a requirement implies a punishment if someone doesn’t fulfill it).

  

          1.3.4.

          Again, P & H’s proposed legislative construct does not necessarily supply the content nor motivation behind moral rules, but only makes them properly obligatory. It supplies normativity.

  

1.4. Realism.

1.4.1.

Clarke, the first realist, replies by pointing out that Hobbes’ assumption of an antecedent acceptance of the social contract as the affirming structure of morality invalidates the rest of his argument. If humanity had a tendency to form a social contract, then why cannot this tendency itself obligate us to behave properly? It certainly must do so, otherwise no such contracts would form, and we wouldn’t obey whatever authority showed up to impose these contracts either.

Pufendorf seemed to answer this by sketching a notion of legitimate authority, but the same argument applies. Humans must already have an intrinsic notion of a “legitimate authority” in order for them to acknowledge one, but this is an assumption not proved by Pufendorf in his explication.

Similar problems afflict Hobbes’ reply to this objection, which is to say that a God’s authority depends not on any of our normative capacities, but strictly his infinite power to sanction and punish sins / wrongdoing.

Deriving authority only from a source of power is in general a bad idea, since anyone without this power cannot be held accountable to this authority.

An example: suppose a moral authority did get its legitimacy from its power to punish. Then if I commit a moral crime and get away with it, did I commit a crime at all? The authority failed to punish me, and if my obligation stemmed from this ability to punish, then I lose my obligation.

A conjugate problem comes with Hume’s morality: that we get our obligations from the strength of our desires. In this case, anyone can use his desire to do anything and call that desire a moral obligation[[1]](https://d.docs.live.net/fe5d9419c2945bf4/Desktop/F2-3-8-18/philosophy/notes/sourcesOfNormativity.docx#_ftn1).

As cited above, deriving moral obligation from pure power is a bad idea.

  

1.4.2.

If these objections lead us to reject voluntarism, then we still have to answer for the explanation behind normative value.

The realist answers this by rejecting an explanatory concept of normativity, instead pointing to it as a simple irreducible fact of the world. Moral obligation exists, and this should therefore motivate us to be moral (p à q).

Richard Price also asserts that, if moral actions do not have such intrinsic natures, then no actions can obligate us at all (~p à ~q).

At points, Samuel Clarke’s own defense of realism amounts to a Kantian conception of obligation: not that it comes as a thing-in-itself, but that it comes from the agent’s own mind, where the agent acts as a thing-in-itself. At other points he aligns with Price in a conception similar to G. E. Moore’s in the 20th century, of obligation and goodness itself as irreducibly real properties of the world.

Rational intuitionism in the 20th century follows the same general lines of argument of Clarke and Price when they agree.

‘Does Moral Philosophy Rest on a Mistake?’ and ‘Duty and Interest’ both hammer home the point that it is senseless to ask why one should be moral. Either a moral reason is provided for one to be moral (circular reasoning), or an irrelevant reason. If moral philosophy uses ‘why should one be moral?’ as its central question, then the whole field has made a mistake.

  

1.4.3.

Where the realist brings the endless regress of “why” questions to a stop by decree, Hume and Kant reiterate the problem. The realist does not actually answer the normative question for anyone seeking reasons to be moral, instead creating some metaphysical construct he would call the good or the obligatory, and simply assert we must believe in it.

  

1.4.4.

When we speak of realism, we’re talking about substantive moral realism in contrast to procedural moral realism, the latter of which most people hopeful about ethics as a subject hold. Procedural moral realism holds that there are certain right or wrong answers to moral questions, and that this is true in virtue of our ability to design procedures to arrive at these truths.

In contrast, substantive moral realists claim that there are right or wrong answers to moral questions because there are right / wrong moral entities already existent in the world, independent of any procedures we conceive to track them.

The respective negation of each would be a kind of moral nihilism, and moral anti-realism, the latter of which still affirms the possibility of answering moral questions, but denies the existence of intrinsic normative entities as answers to them.

  

1.4.5.

John Mackie sums up many cogent criticisms of substantive moral realism (p. 37) by arguing the sheer logical impossibility of objective moral values. Objective moral values / duties would overwhelmingly motivate those acquainted with it, complete with a clear set of principles for fulfilling these values. No such framework exists in the world.

One of Mackie’s strong points here is that, if duty does not motivate someone to act moral, telling the person that it’s his duty to act moral doesn’t really help.

Furthermore, propagating ethical theories to this person (Kant’s FUL, Utilitarianism, Aretaics, etc.) don’t help in addressing the normative question either, since the person asks whether he should do this notwithstanding whether the action is required by morality itself. The question as to whether morality itself is normative still stands.

A difficulty in this conversation is that the normative question can sometimes be conflated with a theoretical question of obligation. Prichard seems to fall into this conflation, where the question ‘is this obligation normative?’ is mixed with ‘is obligation normative?’ as the same question, even though the questions are very different.

What’s worrying is that, along with this conflation, realists obfuscate and refuse to answer the normative question. This is troubling because the existence of intrinsic normative entities in the world must be supported by our belief that we have obligations.

Once we are confident, we can know these entities properly, and this confidence is naively assumed to be the case by moral realists. Nobody actively considering the normative question can be included in the realist’s discussion. Therefore moral realism cannot answer the normative question.

          1.4.6.

Contemporary realists such as Nagel have argued for a reinterpretation of intrinsic goodness, not as distinct or mysterious metaphysical entities, but just as certain truths which regard powerful aspects of life, such as pleasure / pain / duty / desires / rationality, etc. The general problem, however, remains as to how we understand these truths or the reasons used to arrive at these truths. Because Nagel apparently finds them self-evident, there is nothing more to discuss from his end of the normative question.

          1.4.7.

Two massive challenges in answering the normative question seem to be (1) the differences in fundamental assumptions between philosophers, and (2) the conflation of normativity with many other questions, including objectivity, obligation, reason, etc. The second challenge is the more problematic for moral philosophy, because philosophers see fit to ignore the normative question entirely through redefining it in their preferred terminology.

Moore made this mistake in his ‘open question’ argument: no analysis of goodness can settle the questions about whether certain objects have goodness, so it is better to proceed with goodness as an unanalyzable axiom begotten from intuition. This again ignores the normative question, ask we can still ask why we should pursue this goodness at all if we cannot even define it, to which a moral realist would reply with no reply at all (as Clarke / Nagel do in their considerations of the good as self-evidently persuasive).

The normative terms that philosophers use in this space tend to ignore the normative question, so the normative question is presented in a much more generalized form in this book. Once we get past this terminology, general objections to substantive moral realism can be made.

  

1.4.8.

The substantive realist’s answer to why we have normativity is that we can grasp the existence of certain normative ethical concepts in the world, with the assumption that we believe in these entities as soon as we find them.

If someone asks a realist ‘why should I behave this way?’ or ‘why is this obligatory to me?’, the realist would simply reply ‘because that’s just the way it is’, meaning he considers ethics a body of knowledge, a construct existent in the world after creation by humans, a thing self-evidently believed and followed.

And this is the problem: ethics is treated like a systemic, technical, logical subject rather than a subject of practical guide to real-world scenarios.

  

1.4.9.

Another argument that rejects moral realism that CMK doesn’t agree with is that we do not need metaphysical normative constructs to explain moral motives. Moral motives can in fact be explained biologically, psychologically and culturally, without even once referencing these supposedly independent constructs.

We return to a better-formulated version of this argument later. However, a quick separation is needed. This argument seems to work against all forms of moral realism (substantive moral realism given the first argument; practical realism since explanations alternative to our reasons for committing certain actions can be given for why we commit them instead).

Problems with this argument:

(1) It only states that practical reasoning and / or normative entities are not required for moral explanation. But it does not in any way disprove that these elements can be used for moral explanation.

(2) It assumes that explanation / description is the primary role of concepts. It happens that substantive moral realism agrees with this assumption! Hence the force of the argument.

(3) But this is not why we have normative concepts. We have them because we are capable of having them. We ask questions about obligation because we are capable of doing so.

(4) So, it is true that we don’t need metaphysical entities to explain normative morality or why we have it. But this is worse for substantive realism, since it makes the necessary connection between normative entities and normative morality.

Discussion of (5) from above:

(a) Note that CMK in making this argument also seems to argue that this practical consideration acts antecedently to explanatory considerations. Otherwise this argument would not sufficiently reply to the skeptical objection at the beginning of 1.4.9. The reason being that CMK uses our mental capabilities as an explanation for normative concepts; however she also admits that this argument works when we think explanation is an important function of our moral concepts.

(b) Based on the above, it seems that the mental explanation is not really a descriptive explanation. (Assume we take explanation of X to mean a causal reference to some object that describes something about X.) Rather, it is a response to the ‘alternative explanation’ argument, in that it admits ‘yes, there might be other explanations, but all of these other explanations depend on the ability to think about moral questions in the first place. One wouldn’t even be able to ask these questions if he weren’t literally capable of doing so, and this applies to the person making the alternative explanation argument as well.

(c) An example illustrates this: the ‘alternative explanation’ argument could say that evolutionary psychology, physiology, culture, and / or purely normative metaphysical constructs (whatever the hell those are) motivate us to act normatively. This is fine, but the self-consciousness that allows a potential moral agent to even ask the normative question acts antecedently to any of these considerations. Self-consciousness, reflection and evaluation themselves form the nature of our actions much more readily than any subversive biological / cultural machinery does (at least this is what CMK seems to argue).

(d) Note also that this argument seems to attempt to end the ‘why’ regress by placing normative capabilities once and for all within us, conscious animals. Though this needs to be argued much more extensively.

1.5. Conclusions.

1.5.1.

Is the normative questioner asking for knowledge at all? Substantive realists seem to think so, but if we think in the mind of a person asking the question, he is asking to be persuaded at a practical level as to why he should feel obligated to anything a moral philosopher says. The realist rests his beliefs on confidence, not on any sort of actual persuasive discovery of morality, and so has nothing to say to our normative questioner.
