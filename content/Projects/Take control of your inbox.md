Email drains our attention. Use these tactics to gain control over your inbox. 

# Filter your communications
*Sender filters* are messages, explicit surveys, or any mechanisms that serve to filter communications coming to you so that only relevant messages actually get to your attention. 

# Adopt a process-centric approach 
> I’ve attached a draft outline of the paper we discussed. Thoughts?

For messages that *are* relevant and request an open-ended response like above, it’s important to step back and ask the following questions before drafting a response: 

>What is the project represented by this message? What’s the most efficient set of messages for bringing this project to a successful conclusion? What’s the *immediate next step* in this process? 

Take a few minutes to think of a response to each. 

This comprises a **process-centric** approach to email, where you design a hypothetical process for taking this person’s message to a project conclusion and write that process as a message back to them, “closing the loop” on as many open items to be addressed as possible. 

>Hey, great work and thanks for the draft! I’ll read your draft over this week and send back an edited version with my comments by Friday. I’ll let you consider my edits and polish the rest up into a final draft. No need to reply or follow up with me after I return my edits unless there are other issues. 

The above message is concise, yet careful. It outlines the entire procedure from current to final draft and addresses what to do in the event of any contingencies. It is structured. It is sufficiently precise. And it takes the lead on communication. 

Process-centric communication is systematic, almost decision tree-like. This is by design. It renders possibilities, considerations, and decisions concrete, and hence minimizes ambiguity that could waste lots of time.

Of course, you have to actually (promptly) do all of the things you’ve commited to doing in this message. So as you send it, add all those tasks to your calendar, start timeboxing any you want to do today, and make sure the other party is up to speed. 

Then you can get back to thinking and working deeply. 

# Don’t respond 
Don’t respond if any of the following applies to a message:
1. it’s ambiguous or hard to respond clearly to. 
2. it’s not interesting. 
3. nothing great would happen if you responded; nothing terrible would happen if you didn’t.
