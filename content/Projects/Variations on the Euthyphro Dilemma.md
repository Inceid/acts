# Preamble 
This essay describes a generalized form of the Euthyphro Dilemma, a famous dilemma from Plato’s Dialogue *Euthyphro* wherein Socrates asks Euthyphro the following question in an attempt to ascertain the source of moral authority. It is a dialogical attempt to answer the question “what makes a good thing good?”.

# Original Form
**Is the pious being loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because it is being loved by the gods?**

**Modernized:** Does god love something because it is good? Or is something good because god loves it?

# Analysis
The direction of Plato's Euthyphro argues wholeheartedly for the first clause and against the second, by way of arguing that good things are good irrespective of whether divine authorities distinguish them as good. This entails that goodness has an intrinsic quality independent of their appraisal by authorities. 
Suppose we consider for sake of argument that something is good because it is endorsed by the gods or some other authority as good. The canonical next question is "what makes the gods pious, then?" 
- If the response we receive is that the gods don't have to be pious for their opinion of the pious to be valid, that makes no fucking sense. Why would I believe anyone's opinion about the right and the good if they weren't good themselves? This is especially the case for authority figures, who presumably embody the qualities they endorse in objects. 
- If the response we receive is that the gods are pious simply because they are, then again that makes no sense. Why should I listen to an authority just because you tell me I need to listen to them? I'd rather know for what reasons I should obey them, and what qualifications they have. Otherwise I may well be asked to obey the dictates of an evil divine dictator. 
- If the response we receive is that the gods are pious because they embody a certain group of traits that we consider pious traits, which seems to me to be much more common sense (as thereafter we can have a concrete discussion about what makes good things good), then we have admitted that the pious is pious because of some traits outside of its mere endorsement by the gods. This entails something even cooler: we actually don't need to consult the gods at all about what is good, so long as we can isolate the traits of good and evil and study them in the world apart from their endorsement by a divine voice. 

# Institutional Euthyphro
Is the intellectually excellent being loved by academic institutions because it is excellent, or is it intellectually excellent because it is being loved by academic institutions?

Assume for sake of argument the same answer holds here as before. Then clearly institutional endorsement, besides from representing a possible consensus on the matter, doesn't add any value to the value proposition of the excellent. That the excellent is excellent because of independent qualities that we can appreciate and recognize using our own judgment.

# Generalized Euthyphro
Is **desirable X** endorsed by **authority A** because it has **desirable qualities Q**, or does it have **Q** because it is endorsed by **A**?

# Euthyphrian Reductions
An affirmative answer to the first clause of the Euthyphro implies the possibility of recognizing and constructing **Q** independently of the canonical authorities **A** that appraise **X** as having **Q**. I'll move forward on the assumption that it's possible to construct **Q**. 

Does there exist a generalized procedure for observing, investigating, constructing, and implementing **X** with qualities **Q** without adhering to or following the canonical forms of **A**?
* If *yes*, we'll call the procedure a **Euthyphrian Reduction** of **\{X, Q, A\}**. 

For a motivating example for my case, set the following:
* X := a graduate-level degree combining fundamental programs in Philosophy, Computation, Medicine, and Systems (PCMS);
* Q := originality of research, contributivity to the field (combination of "interestingness" and "relevance"), demonstration of mastery of fundamentals;
* A := PhD programs and faculty, a formally approved thesis committee.
call this the **metadegree** instance of the Euthyphro.

A Euthyphrian Reduction of \{X, Q, A\} above would
1. assemble the systems, qualities of excellent research, and evaluative mechanisms for graduate students in each of the major programs listed in X, thereby extracting, refining, and elucidating Q,
2. construct a **reduction** of those systems, or rather a version of those systems that is
	1. adapted to my timeline and current obligations,
	2. adapted to my unique research goals,
	3. adapted to my current set of resources and ability to access further resources in the future. 
3. delete A.

# Appendix: Constructivism and the First Clause
I don't think the first clause can be demonstrated by way of a declarative argument about the inherent nature of good and its relation to authorities that appraise it. An anti-authoritarian answer to the dilemma requires a demonstration.

I'd rather accept the first answer as an axiom in need of practical demonstration anyways, as doing so makes life more exciting. To be able to solve the metadegree instance and thereby demonstrate it as a solution to the Euthyphro is such an exciting prospect, I need to at least try it.
