# Vastness and sandboxiness
The ideal research project will be like my favorite games, books, and movies: sprawling, vast, challenging, open-world adventures with many different kinds of challenges, people, opportunities to learn deep skills, and an opportunity to experience and drive an epic journey. I like a good sandbox-style project - a vast landscape where I can experiment with many different combinations of hypotheses and see what comes out of them. 

It is extremely challenging to find a project with direct clinical relevance that satisfies the above. Most of the things I find deep abiding interest in today are of a much more abstract quality. They have more to do with mathematical models, complex systems, and philosophical and metaphysical ideas about personality, agency, morality and moral judgment, and individuality. I’m drawn to these ideas a lot more than any direct clinical or field-specific questions in medicine. 

# The Abstract, the Strange
Moreover, oftentimes I find that the research that I like to read about is quite different than the research I like to do. I spend a lot of time reading about antimicrobial resistance, clinical guidelines in cancer treatment, new precision genomics/medicine-based treatment algorithms and datasets, advances in biosecurity and public health, and other sprawling vast interesting frontier fields. But that doesn’t usually translate to me wanting to make a contribution to those fields. Conversely, the “research” (maybe better stated as “loose academic work” or “drafts”) that I produce rather than consume is much more abstract, strange, sometimes nonsensical, often non-medical. I write a lot about systems medicine and ways to apply fundamental concepts in computer science to reasoning about fundamental concepts in physiology and pathophysiology. 

I write a lot about things like political medicine, postcynicism, the generic methods of medicine, metaphysical states that promote flow states, different “isms” that I come up with and shove into my notebooks, everyday observations about my experiences. Sometimes I see something that I get really obsessed about and I produce something cool. In all cases, what shuts the feedback loops guiding these projects down is when I ask for advice on what to do with them. People tell me to not worry about publishing, or to try to do more clinical research when I have time. Clinical research is empty calories. I don’t want to produce more of that junk. 

The last research project I did, at Dana Farber, was a very mixed experience. I got a lot of valuable advice on how to improve the quality of my presentations, how to advance my specific project, and ways to intelligently and strategically interrogate hypotheses about my datasets. I really enjoyed assembling and running my analysis suite. I enjoyed presenting my work and hearing people’s thoughts on it. I enjoyed lab meetings. I did not enjoy much of the work when I wasn’t focused. I also did not enjoy crafting my poster. I didn’t enjoy thinking about the impact (or lack thereof) of my work. Cancer is incredibly interesting; I’m not sure computational oncology, though, is my future. 

So now I’m stuck. I would like to move beyond medical research. I don’t care to do research for the sake of getting into any specific specialty. I don’t care enough about doing specific procedures to want to spend too much time on them - I’m in medicine to develop relationships with people. If anything, I’m in medicine because I see medicine as an intrinsically social and political profession, one whose fate is deeply tied to the fate of the government that regulates and licenses it. And I would rather spend my time either doing things that build my power in a concrete sense or strengthening my intellectual capacities in a generic manner. So I’d want to engage in projects that make me a better thinker and executor of ideas, period. I’m not personally tied to any specific causes or fields other than perhaps psychiatry for those reasons. 

# Moving forward
That being said, “a vast sandbox” and “the abstract and strange” are two quite peculiar and specific descriptors of the kind of research I would enjoy, and I think specific preferences generally portend good things for my ability to find/design a project in the future.
