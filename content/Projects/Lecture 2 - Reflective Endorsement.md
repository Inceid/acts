# 2.1. Introduction.

## 2.1.1.
We have normative problems because it’s part of our reflective nature. We seek to answer normative questions like the general one posed at the beginning of lecture I with secure philosophical foundations because we’re worried that whatever morality we conjured up might not survive this reflective tendency in the end.

But if we take reflection seriously and find that we might endorse moral claims after doing so, then morality will be normative. The view that supports this process is called reflective endorsement.

## 2.1.2.
This sort of view appears as a natural objection by sentimentalists against rationalists in the 18th century, as well as a natural objection against realism. We wouldn’t say in this view that we approve of an action because it is good, but that an action is good because we approve of it.

If this view is true, then normative morality depends entirely on human sentiments, not on the nature of moral acts themselves, and now seeking truth-values for our moral clams is useless.

In a significant way, this agent-centered theory of normativity is shared by several anti-realists throughout history, and by Kant himself in his description of a normative morality. However, Kant’s logical conclusions are very different from the others’, and CMK argues that these conclusions are the correct ones.

## 2.2. David Hume.
### 2.2.1.
An interesting question in light of this discussion is where the normative question belongs in Hume’s philosophy. Hume uses the constructs of a theoretical philosopher to provide explanatory adequacy for our moral sentiments, and a practical philosopher to use the tools of rhetoric, emotion and persuasion to propagate such sentiments in the populace, to make them appealing and desirable.

But where does the person who wants to supply normative adequacy fit here? There’s the quiet, unpersuasive systematic theorist and the glib politician, but who is the normative answerer?

Many interpretations may arise. Perhaps Hume simply wishes to denigrate moral philosophy as an abstruse art prone to abuse by charlatans. CMK’s own view is that normative adequacy cannot be provided within either of these two constructs, but arises when they interact.

To be direct, if the theoretician’s discoveries are truthfully propagated by the practical philosopher, then these discoveries are sufficiently normative, since there is an agreement between the two philosophical constructs. This theory claims to be normative.

### 2.2.2.
Note that reflective endorsement might lead to rejection of moral behavior from any number of views. Someone might reject moral claims because morality might be found paradoxical, defeating, or unsatisfactory from the point of view of someone who buys into that morality in the first place[[2]](https://d.docs.live.net/fe5d9419c2945bf4/Desktop/F2-3-8-18/philosophy/notes/sourcesOfNormativity.docx#_ftn2). In Hume’s case, morality is assessed from the point of view of (a) self-interest and (b) this ‘moral sense’ itself.

### 2.2.3.
Hume claims we make moral judgments according to two kinds of sentiments. The first is the personal, where we feel sympathy for a person and judge their character at a personal level. The second is the general, where we measure their conduct against general norms. Both types of sentiments give our moral sentiments a sort of objectivity, as we can agree or disagree about each of them and reach some resolution on the person being judged.

Such judgements are fundamentally based in the pleasure and pain that they reference / generate. Pleasure and pain gives us certain powerful desires, some of which may override rational deliberation, to act in certain normative ways.

Good, the explanatory question was answered. Now the normative question in this sense asks whether we should really yield to these desires, and care about having good character.

Not much is offered in argument for an affirmative answer to this by Hume, but he might not need much. Hume simply refers to the agent’s propensity to prefer qualities and desires that are useful to him, and that morally good or benevolent desires just feel better than bad or vicious ones. So, people will just naturally act in this way whether they consciously agree to it or not.

One might think this explanation avoids the issue of obligation in the strictest sense, but this is wrong. Self-interest and the moral sense inform the systems of duty and obligation that society constructs for itself, so people will still perform just actions out of duty. It’s just that the grounding for this duty is something relevant to the agent’s own moral capacities.

This provides a motivation to not be bad. To act badly would lead to disapproving of oneself.

But then the question of a ‘sensible knave’ comes up. If a man deliberates over acting immorally but such that his actions do not significantly undermine his self-interest in that they destroy or harm the system of justice that protects his self-interest in the first place, then can he do so? Hume’s answer is to take Clarke / Nagel’s route of ignoring the knave and proceeding to congratulate the honorable folk who wouldn’t even think of doing such a thing.

Interestingly, Hume’s theory does promote a kind of reply that refers to self-disapproval and humility upon conducting this immoral act. The fact that this honorable crowd will disapprove of the sensible knave’s actions might well provide an incentive against the knave acting badly. Hume’s reply, which at first seemed to take the form ‘you should disapprove of your actions because honorable people disapprove of your actions’ is not circular. So, the knave cannot answer ‘but why should I be honorable?’, as the risk of humility and disapproval by honorable folk will disrupt his self-interest and moral sense enough that he might reconsider.

Because humans seem to be sympathetic creatures in reality, this reply has a point. Disapproval by others can lead to disapproval of ourselves.

- (Comment) It has yet to be addressed, however, when and how one might identify a crowd to be dishonorable. Hume might well identify a moral crowd as one that is socially harmonious, polite, and respective within itself, but are these qualities alone sufficient to generate a moral society? Do they lead to other moral qualities arising?

### 2.2.4.
Two criticisms from moral realists arise from these considerations.

One is that, since we should praise virtue for its own sake and not because virtuous actions serve our interest, Hume’s argument is irrelevant.

However, Hume’s actually saying that it is in our interest to praise / practice virtue for its own sake. It is an argument that concerns interest because this is the starting point – human beings are interested creatures, so it’s better to speak in the language of interest when we answer the normative question.The Butlerian argument proceeds similarly – we don’t care about the interests you already have (the realist would assume one already has an interest to be moral), we care about changing your interests, and persuading you to adopt moral ones. Moral interests-in-themselves are good for you to have.

Discussion of the above points:

(a) At a glance, this view seems to be nonsense. If you adopt moral interests-in-themselves based on your self-interest, then you in fact are not adopting moral interests-in-themselves. How can you adhere to virtue for its own sake if you adopted it because it was in your interest to do so? It seems that practicing virtue for its own sake and considering it because of self-interest are contradictory maxims.

(b) I’m not sure how this plays out given the rest of CMK’s analysis. Since she has rejected the realist view that proving metaphysical normative entities into existence can answer the normative question, does she then not see a problem with interests / sentiments acting antecedently, and thus potentially cancelling out, beliefs in virtue-in-itself? That is, since we cannot accept normative things-in-themselves as answers to the normative question, must we then turn to our interest and say ‘this is the only way you can believe in a moral concept anyways’? It just seems to me that “it is in our interest to practice virtue for its own sake” is an odd thing to say.

(c) The most reasonable interpretation is that Korsgaard takes sympathetic / empathetic / benevolent sentiments to act antecedently to any moral consideration. If this is her argument, then it would have to be the case that someone must be keenly interested in his own mind in order to be moral in any sense, whether for morality’s own sake or just for his self-interest.

CMK claims that such an analysis promotes harmony between moral and selfish interests.

The second objection holds that, even if this theory gives a plausible set of reasons for being moral, it only persuades us to be moral at an extrinsic level (this is an objection cited within the comments of the previous discussion on Hume). We would like to show that morality is intrinsically normative and / or intrinsically good, not that which depends on something else such as self-interest or human nature.

A certain view of Hume’s may answer this.

### 2.2.5.
The view is called ‘normativity as reflexivity’, which is implied in his Treatise of Human Nature. The view forms a general account of normative claims that a mental faculty is capable of producing normative judgments if it can, by itself and with the appropriate objects under consideration, generate some positive normative claim about a state of the world.

Hume asserts that the understanding cannot do this (the understanding refers to the capabilities of humans to logically analyze and categorize the world around them). When the understanding takes itself with its objects of analysis, it ‘subverts itself’, diminishing its own legitimacy as it recognizes the limits of its own jurisdiction over reality. In this sense, the understanding cannot use itself to make moral judgments – it is a completely self-defeating faculty.[[3]](https://d.docs.live.net/fe5d9419c2945bf4/Desktop/F2-3-8-18/philosophy/notes/sourcesOfNormativity.docx#_ftn3)

However, the ‘moral sense’ Hume repeatedly references does pass this test. Contrary to the understanding, reflection on the moral sense itself (along with its objects of consideration) self-perpetuates the moral sense. Such reflection also therefore strengthens our moral convictions, since the moral sense is a primary generator of those convictions.

Discussion on the above points:

(a) I would agree with CMK that this is what Hume seems to have meant in his comparison between the understanding and the moral sense.

(b) For what it’s worth, I also agree with Hume about the fact that sentiments always seem to self-perpetuate, whereas reason always seems to self-diminish, when they are applied to themselves respectively.

(c) But I would disagree about the nature of these self-affects. Desires may self-perpetuate, but they do so without any critical direction, without any resistance except from the understanding or from other people. They show a very different sort of progression than reason’s self-diminishing, which is itself calculated and regulated.

(d) An example would help at this point. We might reflect on the fact that we cannot prove objects to be real entities to us, leading to a Brain-in-a-vat-like problem. But practical reasoning at this point immediately enters, and we tell ourselves ‘what’s the point of this? I cannot prove I am not a brain in a vat, but is it better that I spend time reflecting on it, or take the most practical course of action given this lack of knowledge?’ Many other practical reasons exist to pull us away from such reflections, such as our friends, family, their opinions on reality, and the posterity that we affect through our actions.

(e) So yes, reason might lead us to doubt our fundamental assumptions and axioms about the way we see the world, but it knows when to stop. We generally know when to stop ourselves. It’s the sentiments, the desires, that do not know this. That’s why their proliferation is much more pernicious than the diminishing of reason.


### 2.2.6.
Hutcheson anticipates a rationalist objection against the moral-sense-normativity view of Hume’s, namely that we must judge the moral sense to be good or bad, but can only do so with regard to some faculty external to the moral sense itself. His response also seems to have inspired Hume’s own conception of normative morality.

The general argument asks if X decides what is moral and immoral, then what decides whether X itself is moral or not? Surely it cannot be X – how can we be sure that X does not bias itself in its self-judgment? And the infinite regress returns. This is what prompts the original realist response.

Hutcheson responds by arguing that we require some point of view from which we can coherently judge things as good or bad, and that these points of view are limited in number. There is no possible way to leave all perspectives that allow judgment and then still ask whether something is good or bad. So there must be some point of view from which we judge goodness or badness that we can stomach as a species if normative morality is to survive. Hence Hume’s inspiration for the reflexivity test.

### 2.2.7.
Now to answer the realist’s extrinsic-intrinsic objection directly: the reflexivity test for moral acceptance that Hume implements actually comes quite close to proving intrinsic normative values; namely, that human nature itself is intrinsically normative.

Realists cannot coherently reject this view if Hutcheson’s argument holds. For if morality could not be normatively challenged and / or produced within the faculties that make up human nature, there is no other point of view from which we can coherently assess it. From every view, we see it fit to embrace human nature, and see no view from which we might reject it. So human nature has normative authority over us. Hence an agreement between classical Greek aretaic conceptions of the good and the sentimentalist formulation of the same conception.

## 2.3. Bernard Williams.
### 2.3.1.
In Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy, Bernard Williams tries to formulate such an aretaic conception amenable to modern moral philosophy – the ‘congruence’ that Rawls mentions[[4]](https://d.docs.live.net/fe5d9419c2945bf4/Desktop/F2-3-8-18/philosophy/notes/sourcesOfNormativity.docx#_ftn4) is an apt tool for comparison. Where we might use a substantive realist stance to test congruence between our statements about the world and the world itself (‘itself’ meaning how it appears to human modes of perception), we must use something else entirely for testing ethical claims. This is where reflective endorsement comes in.

### 2.3.2.
Williams assesses the same problem proposed by Hutcheson and accepted by Hume regarding point of view, and takes the scenario in terms of ‘conceptual schemes’ that we use to assess the world or ethical concepts. The normative question does not ask whether these schemes have been correctly applied, but whether they are adequate in an overall sense. The former question only requires self-verification, the latter something else entirely.

Williams also makes a distinction between the world-in-itself and the way we perceive it by using an asymptotic “absolute conception” of the world as a backdrop. Against this backdrop, we assess the dependency of our worldly-concepts on our own perspectives.

Intuitively, the concept of color is much more perspective-dependent than the concept of wavelengths of light. This would therefore mean that the closer a concept comes to the absolute conception of the world, the more perspective-independent it is by nature.

Two other properties inform us of perspective-independence.
- For one, if a theory of concepts can be explained by another theory of concepts closer to the absolute conception, then this closer theory will justify our original theory of concepts as validly referring to a perspective-independent reality. If a wavelength theory of light explains the color theory of light, then the color theory is a valid way of perceiving the world.
- Secondly, concepts can be more perspective-independent if they can be ‘peer-reviewed’, that is, if they can be shared with other creatures with different modes of perception than our own.
- These three properties entail that the conception closest to the absolute conception is that which is most perspective-independent.

### 2.3.3.
There are problems with this view, most notably the question: why are human concepts more perspective-independent than human perceptions? We expect Williams to respond that perceptive judgments are more perspective-dependent than conceptions because conceptions can be used by themselves to explain perceptive judgments. The order of explanation then forms the order of perspective-independence; the farther we go up the ladder of generalizability in explanation, the more perspective-independent our theory becomes. (The progression is conducted via the scientific method in Williams’ example.)

The issue is that this solution presupposes that science tells us about the absolute conception of the world in the first place, and such a function has not been proven except through science itself. If someone were to doubt that science tells us about this conception, then Williams’ arguments wouldn’t answer his doubts. We set this worry aside to deal with later.

### 2.3.4.
So we understand that the key convergence in science is that between scientific concepts and the world-in-itself as it really is (or at least as far as we can get to it). What, then, is the parallel for ethics?

While ethics has no parallels for perceptive judgments within the realm of ethics, he does make a distinction between ‘thin’ ethical judgments (things like good, ought, evil) that do not appear to be world-guided, and ‘thick’ ethical judgments (things like brutality, cowardice, lying, gratitude) that do appear to be world-guided and at the same time ethical / normative in their nature. An action motivated by fear above all else can be called cowardly as a matter of fact, and in most cases to call an action cowardly is to say that one ought not do it, as is the example CMK gives.

A relevant objection from a prescriptivist / emotivist on this issue is that such ‘thick’ concepts explain nothing factual, but only our sentiments about some situation. In this sense, they are no more action-guiding than our ‘thin’ concepts.

Now we cannot quantitatively or accurately apply these ‘thick’ judgements like we can with perceptions, but these form pretty basic building blocks for anyone to make their way through the ethical world. In this sense, they can be analogous to perceptual judgments.

### 2.3.5.
We can judge similar ethical propositions using the same line of argument. While a monk may say a certain act is sinful, a knight that it is dishonorable, a politician say it is corrupt, and while all of these may mean different things, there is a collective sense in which all three of these actors imply that one ought not do these things. This is the convergence class of such ethical claims – the ought question.

- (Comment) It is claimed here, and CMK seems to endorse this view, that all ethical claims can be boiled down to some set of ought claims. This needs to be examined closely, because if successful, this suggestion moves us much closer to unifying normativity as a concept-in-itself.

### 2.3.6.
We might after all think that the scientific analogy doesn’t really hold. Whereas all doctors share the general questions about optimizing health and well-being, not all people share goals or maxims about morality. This runs the risk of delegitimizing any judgment we attempt to make about the other person.

This is where the test of reflective endorsement comes into play. By analyzing human nature and its moral faculties, we find in it the only possible tool through which we can maximize human well-being through belief in morals. This argument would have nothing to say about the intrinsic truth or goodness of those propositions.

### 2.3.7.
Williams in this line of argument would generally agree with the anti-realist stance that we project ethical values onto the world. Pure perspective-independent ethical sentiments aren’t informative about morality at all, and are anyways unattainable. What remains to be shown is whether it is good to have those sentiments in the first place; Williams shows this by showing congruence with human flourishing, whereas Hume shows this by congruence with self-interest.

### 2.3.8.
Note that the difference between Hume and Williams here is that Hume is talking about values necessarily congruent to human nature, whereas Williams is talking about values that might be offered differently from different cultures. Williams’ reflective test, then, in a way is more generalizable than Hume’s, and is at less of a threat from destruction by mankind if they find such a test useless to their means.

2.3.9.

An element of reflexivity also presides in Williams’ view, that is to say, that any answer by an agent to the normative question must satisfy that agent’s own interests in being ethical. This, again, could be conflated with things like egoism that are irrelevant to the discussion; an agent considering ethics from an ethical point of view should find their answer satisfying in order to act ethically.

Bottom line for Williams and Hume, if we can judge our ethical positions as good from every plausible point of view that demands practical actions that we can offer, including the ethical positions’ point of view themselves, then we can say we have established these ethical positions as normative for us.

## 2.4. John Stuart Mill.
### 2.4.1.

Mill also uses reflective endorsement in his views. He distinguishes fundamentally between an external sanction, which would usually take the form of a reward or punishment from the physical world, and an internal sanction, which would take the form of a mental reminder. He states that the principle of utility bears both kinds of sanctions, but that neither of them have anything to do with the proof for the principle that he cites in Utilitarianism. The proof is there for consumption, but it will not motivate one to be moral.

### 2.4.2.
Once again, the normative question doesn’t seem to fit anywhere. The proof offers explanatory adequacy (if plausible), but the sanctions seem more of a training tool than provisions for normative adequacy. His own writing further obfuscates this confusion as to where we get moral obligation itself from.

A quick turn will help traverse the discussion: all philosophers discussed so far, excluding Mill, are ‘internalists’. That is to say, they all believe that if and when a person feels moral duties and considerations, they also have by that virtue moral motives for acting on those duties. Mill himself seems to be an externalist – he says that, were there someone who was not brought up in an environment that encourages development of the proper external / internal sanctions, then this person could not ask the normative question at all. In this sense he argues that training for sanction-development acts antecedently to the normative question.

### 2.4.3.
It should be clear that the utilitarian sense of obligation, which involves training a normative agent with the proper internal / external sanctions in line with principles supporting the principle of utility, is also self-perpetuating through reflection. Even more so, the lack of certain internal sanctions like sociability, community or individuality, one would be forced to reflect on this apparent lacking and would reason that the void caused by this lacking should be filled with the proper sanction.

Thus this conception answers the normative question coherently.

## 2.5. The Reflective Agent.
### 2.5.1.
We notice many issues after a bit of reflection here.

Why exactly should anyone care (a crude way to put it, but seriously.)? Surely, if you’re raised like a Utilitarian, you will then become one! What of those who have not been raised with ‘the proper sanctions’? Isn’t the normative question concerned with them as well? What can they do other than seek the exact kinds of reflections that Mill delineates? Can any of Mill’s arguments persuade a non-utilitarian to become one?

We might assume that an affirmative answer to the last question is indeed possible. But then why wouldn’t his proof for the principle of utility persuade us in this way (indeed, he himself notes that this is the case)?

And if these arguments cannot motivate us, what will? Surely, the person who lacks sociability, etc. can only respond to this lack if it is an antecedently instilled sanction at some point within the person’s mind. If someone pops up (and there are many such people) who does not have these behaviors already within him, and this is the answer we give him, how can anyone say utilitarianism withstands the normative question past a surface-level analysis?

The central problem here is that Mill addresses a utilitarian-in-training, not a rational agent asking the normative question. And it definitely doesn’t do so in the first-person. So the normative question is left unanswered.

### 2.5.2.
Consider the sensible knave example again; this will highlight the effects of inadequate defenses of reflection. A person might consider an action thought to be unjust according to first principles, but the action might bring about good consequences in terms of more basic ethical building blocks – it may promote integrity and / or confidence, but might violate the principle of utility, internal / external sanctions, or even the virtuous character that Hume uses.

Even if this is the case, the normative morality that has engendered this problem within our agent has failed this agent. The agent does not have good groundings for supporting his current moral thinking, and so his morality is not really normative.

### 2.5.3.
This difficulty seems to arise in Hume’s consideration of moral sentiments themselves – that they apply as general a priori rules for conduct, and not necessarily to specific situations. And should our defector apply reflective endorsement to his particular situation, or go by the rules word by word?

Of course, the agent should use reflective endorsement whenever he reaches a moral dilemma, be it a general consideration or practical scenario (and the two can very often contradict each other. The implications of such contradictory natures form a whole different discussion.).

Now this forms Kant’s own test for the reflective, autonomous agent. The agent should constantly reflect on his particular situation when deciding how to act. Whereas Williams and Hume use reflective endorsement as a mere exercise for establishing general normative rules, Kant uses reflective endorsement as a living, breathing model of a normative morality, and does so in a much more active manner than Williams / Hume.
