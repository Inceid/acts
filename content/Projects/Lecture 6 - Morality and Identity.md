>Raymond Geuss’s reply and commentary on CMK’s theses.

CMK distinguishes between the ‘categorical imperative’ [CI] and the ‘moral law’ [ML] in lecture 3, where CI imposes minimal conditions on freedom and ML further specifies that free choices vetted by CI must also be agreeable to rational beings when cooperating. Kant does not make the distinction. CMK (1) defends Kant on his free will stance and (2) completes his argument on the moral stance.

(1) Now as the Kantian (that is, philosophers following Kant’s general line of moral thinking in this case) puts it, we are forced by our nature to constantly assess our desires and sensations using our will. But whatever this will is, it can’t depend on our desires, since in that case we wouldn’t be reflecting on ourselves or our rules but only on what we perceive sense. But what is the principle on which the will operates, then?

CMK says reflective endorsement, and applies the notion of a personal, practical identity to this principle. Importantly, Kant settles with CI, and says nothing of identity in his Critiques, and some criticism can be made along this line. If our normative nature depends on a conception of practical identity, then CI alone is not sufficient to supply normative nature to us, since practical identity involves much more than simple universalizability tests.

(2) So, self-conception in some sense is required for identifying with and obeying the laws one makes through CI & ML. CMK reconstructs the Kantian project in terms of this first-person identification, and uses this to claim that she can persuade someone else to value what she values. But the Kantian would never purport to say this, especially in the context of valuing certain objects or qualities. CMK further obfuscates the discussion by oscillating between exceptionally strong (in terms of what they require of the person considering them) definitions of obligation and exceptionally weak ones, and doesn’t seem to take the time to substantively distinguish between them or how the reflective consciousness recognizes their difference.

CMK presents (2) as an alternative to a realist conception of the good, but her argument can fall to the same objections that she herself raises to the realist. Another more pertinent fact is the intersubjective criticism – I can be okay with my reasons, you with yours, but how do I convince you to change yours? CMK responds by redefining reason into the intrinsically public category, but there’s a problem. Granted that reasons are in principle public entities and we therefore can and usually share normative reasons with each other, it does not follow that we must engage in common reasoning with others.

So it doesn’t really matter what is a moral fact, a good idea, a divine command, a determination of the will, or whatever else someone can concoct. In any case, there doesn’t seem to be any way to universally and unequivocally bind us in obligation to one another in all contexts, lest we abandon the general Kantian conception and settle for a much more casuistic, empirical, context-dependent one.
