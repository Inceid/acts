# Preamble
>Why don’t I like doing practice problems? 
>Why don’t I like deliberately, methodically tracing through my code? 
>Why don’t I like focusing on the simple fundamentals? 
>Why can’t I tolerate mastering simple model conceptual systems even with the knowledge that mastering those systems will optimize my real-world performance?

Part of this is because I’m habitually impatient. I want to get to the meat of things, to the “real deal”, immediately - often prematurely. And when I try out the “real deal”, I find that I’m woefully unprepared for it. I try my hand at designing a processor, a compiler, a cancer research project for “realsies” and I find that there’s a shit ton of things I don’t know or have never even thought of considering learning that would have been great to know prior to diving in.

# The Solution: Exercises and Model Systems as Controllers
One idea has been changing my mindset about this a lot: the concept of controllers from systems theory.

When a book asks you to do a practice problem or implement a “toy system”; when you do a practice exam; when you solve a simplification of any problem; all of these are instances of you mastering and familiarizing yourself with a model system that acts as a [controller](Controllers.md) for a much larger system.

When I implement an extremely simple function in a new programming language, I’m establishing a controller for any other function I write: the naming convention, the style and comments, the logic. When I answer a question on a medical school exam, I’m establishing a controller for any other question I answer on every other exam, on rounds to an attending physician, and to my patients when they ask me questions. 

For so long, I was frustrated that I kept hitting these artificial obstacles and was never getting to the “real world”. But now I’ve started to think of all the model systems I’m asked to use as potential effective controllers for the real-world systems I want to understand and manipulate. 

Programming exercises are controllers for solving problems in real-world software implementations. Simple language and machine descriptions like [Y86-64](<CSAPP Chapter 4>) or $\mathbf{E}$ as implemented in [PFPL](<Annotation of Practical Foundations for Programming Languages.md>) aren’t real - no modern computer or language implements them - but they function as conceptual bridges to contemporary systems. 

If I think of these systems - and the exercises that I do to test my understanding of them - as controllers and bridges, then I find it much easier and much more motivating to focus on them for longer periods.
