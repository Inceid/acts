# Notation. 
`memoratio` *memoratio sequence*

# Definition. 
A **memoratio** is a thought experiment whereby we simulate ourselves on our deathbed, imagine what we cherish and regret the most, and work backwards from the deathbed to the experiences that were or will be our most formative and powerful. The sequence of experiences or realizations inferred from working backwards is the "memoratio sequence".

This serves as a generative and agentic tool for deciding what kinds of actions might be worth pursuing to attain a life without regrets. 

# Example. 
1. Imagine you’re on your deathbed. What made your life as good as it was? What do you regret not having done? What mistakes do you really wish you hadn’t made? 

2. Now walk backwards and tell yourself, your real self, all of those things, and start life again, knowing what you know about the future.

# Working backwards from death 
*Memoratio* admits of a continuation which we can call “working backwards from death”. It follows the example above: you’re lying in a hospital bed. It’s a little hard to breathe. Your body’s been failing you for the last few months, but it’s worse today than usual. All you have is the residual energy of your mind. All you can do now is wonder how things might have been different, whether they should have been different. And you ruminate on the past. 

Through the eyes of the dying self, your job at this point is to rewind, in as excruciating detail as possible, from the moment of death back to the present moment. 

The proceeding discussion may seem confusing given its use of present and past tense. When it is used below, present tense refers to the perspective of you in your deathbed, running back your life history. The past tense refers to moments in that simulated life history. 

I strongly advise anyone who does a memoratio to complete all of these steps in the order presented. Stopping in the middle has caused serious problems for me (e.g. systematically listing regrets but not completing the deregret process, hence the regrets lingering in my mind and messing up my emotional state).

## Identify inflection points 
Identify key inflection points in your life: decisions you made that changed the trajectory of the rest of your decisions. Perhaps where you went to college/grad school, who you chose to marry, your most important professional accomplishments or events, a trip you took to some other location that taught you something important and fundamental about yourself. 

Perhaps these inflection points are internal: when you realized something important about a philosophy you’ve been trying to understand; a video you watched about life purpose; a sudden breakthrough after many weeks of reflection on a problem in your life or work. Whatever it is, you can likely point out an inflection point when you encounter it in someone else’s life story. 

## Expand each inflection point into counterfactuals 
For each inflection point, what other decisions could you have made among the options that you had at the time? List out these options and think to yourself whether or not you would have plausibly chosen an alternative. 

## List regrets and resolutions 
For those decisions that you decide there was another plausible option that would have been at least as good, list them under your regrets. 

For the remaining decisions, list them as resolutions. These have passed your internal review. 

Once you have completed this step, **you must complete the remainder of the memoratio**.

## Turn [regret into deregret](<Regret and Deregret>)
>Still lying in bed, you wonder if there may be another chance in the next life to start over and do things the right way. 

For each regret, transform it into a resolution to convert the regret into a “deregret”, a process for acting on the regret and implementing it in your life.
