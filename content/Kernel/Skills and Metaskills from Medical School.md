>Medical school teaches you nothing. It puts a daunting mountain of tedious nonsensical tasks in front of you and asks you to marshall the resources yourself to accomplish it, all while pretending that it is teaching you to do what you taught yourself. But by accomplishing it, you slay many dragons and learn many skills. 

# Preamble 
This is a compendium of important lessons I've learned about myself going through medical school. The most important thesis of this essay is that, by itself, a medical school curriculum teaches you nothing. It's how you interact with and respond to a medical school curriculum that teaches you important lessons. 

# This is *all* a mind game
Medical school is first and foremost, as any other advanced graduate or professional degree program of a similar length is, a mind game. It will challenge your mental fortitude, your resilience, your focus, your concentration, your discipline, your ego, your sense of ethics and integrity, your humanity.

Diagnostic reasoning in the clinical setting is an implementation of [rapid investigation](<Rapid Research>), the process of taking a problem, posing it as a question to investigate and developing a hypothesis or proposal for the question in a rapid, structured manner. 

# Advanced Memorization
For better or for worse, working in any information-dense field requires efficient factual recall. We don't have all the time in the world to learn and recall this information, yet we need to recall it. 

To this end, certain techniques help for memorizing and deeply encoding large swathes of information. You figure out which of these techniques work by applying them on a regular basis towards learning medicine. 

# Proactivity
To learn medicine properly and get good evaluations, a medical student has to be proactive. This entails that they be the first to act in ambiguous situations, especially those involving ambiguous or unclear expectation 

# Decisiveness
Coupled with proactivity is decisiveness, the quality of being able to make, justify, and stand by a decision that may have deep stakes. 

# Representation-Independence
By navigating medical school successfully, you learn to decouple your mind’s activities and dependencies from representations. 

Many times while I was in the office or in the OR just standing, I would wish that something was happening that I could do. I was just sitting there, but my mind crackled with overwhelming energy - I often felt like I was going to explode. But I was in the office, I was at the hospital - I couldn’t just be working on a personal project all the time!

Earlier in medical school, I would sit in lectures wishing they could be more interesting than they were; wishing they were about topics that I cared about. Grand systems-level topics, topics about ethics and our obligations as physicians, when these come into conflict. 

At some point in M2, I realized that I could think about whatever I wanted, whether or not I was sitting in a lecture hall. I could think about philosophical problems and completely tune the lecture out. Previously, I wasn’t accustomed to doing this. My mind’s thoughts up to this point were far more *representation-dependent* - they operated primarily in response to the context and setting I was in. But given how bored I was, I decided to start spawning my own lines of thinking independently and following them instead of trying to follow a droning lecture.

# Appendix: Practical Affirmations
## This path is the best path 
Upon examining different kinds of people completing training under different kinds of graduate programs, I'm fairly convinced that:
1. Most graduate programs (MS, PhD) *do not*, within the formal boundaries of their programs' structures, teach students how to teach, do research, create excellent intellectual work. Far too much is left to the student to implement themselves, with the advisor offering feedback on interim products once in a while but never offering any actual workflow structure or system.
2. The MD curriculum, by virtue of its astonishing degree of breadth in its scientific/factual education, extensive practical team-based education under significant stress, and exposure to a massive variety of fields and specialties that align with its mission, prepares students well to enter any field of their choosing, whether it be traditional medical practice or anything else.

This path is the best path. Given my circumstances, it makes no fucking sense to have done an MD/PhD or MD/MPH. How could I possibly know whether I want to be a research / academic leader, a public health / health systems leader, when the experiences I've had are all broken simulacra of the real deal? The undergrad projects were simulacra - they weren't real. The postbac projects were also all simulacra. They were controlled and contrived to fit a certain timeline. Even the medical school projects are mere simulacra. My timeline will never enable me to do real research.

But much more so than other degrees, by completing the MD I'll have the sheer work ethic to do whatever else I want to do, and see patients once in a while too.

## Do not psychologically block yourself
The medical education system, and Tufts is no exception, will feed you all kinds of propaganda to keep you in its clutches. It will make you think patient care is the only ethical and possible course of action for you. Don't listen to these people. They accelerate the profession's demise by lying to its students. 

## Seek new communities if your current ones don't serve you
I don't want to hang around people oozing learned helplessness. 

## The best way to autonomize and free yourself is to **take your time**
Remember that impatience is taught; it is taught by your system. If you want to break free from its clutches and from the clutches of every other social and intepersonal system that blocks your realization of your potential, you *need* to calm down and take your time. Act intentionally and deliberately.

All of this energy can't go to waste. But it can't be expressed in its current form. It's too scattered, too volatile, too anxious, too frantic. Too undisciplined. That energy can be transformed into something much more refined, with time.
