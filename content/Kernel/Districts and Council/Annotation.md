# Definition 
An **annotation** is a running informal presentation of a text or set of ideas. 

We say an annotation is a *groundwork* annotation if it lays a foundation for many other kinds of projects and thought. In other words, it's a *fundamental* annotation. 

# Implementation
The simplest way to annotate a text is to [sit with a pen and paper while reading it](<Syngenesis>). In practice, however, it helps to adopt a systematic structure for compiling and interpreting a text's content, especially for ease of future use and reference. I delineate some practices that have helped me below. 
## First Pass
### Sketch 
First, sketch (map) the text. Write each header (chapter title or major section) and, if time permits, a brief sentence describing the section's content. 

>[!info] Block quotes
You can also use blockquotes like this to describe general patterns in the surrounding section, pick out examples, asides, or other helpful comments for your readers.

This has several advantages - first and foremost, it chunks the information into digestible boli. 

### Topological Sort 
Topologically sort the text. Figure out what the dependencies are: do you need to read each chapter in the order provided in the text? Can you skip some chapters to get the gist? 

In practice, this is done mentally or during the sketch. For especially challenging books, it may be worth explicitly outlining dependencies prior to diving in. 

### Set your tempo 
Set a regular tempo for reading each section. Typically, texts adopt a certain style that allows readers to recognize unstated patterns of presentation simply by engaging with the text for a sustained period. 

Do whatever keeps your attention on the text. If taking a break after each section to reflect helps you refocus your attention, it's probably the right way to read. If reading several chapters then stopping for a few months is what allows you to regain interest, it's probably also the right way to read. 

After a first pass is done, the annotation is labelled “v1”.

## Second Pass 
A first pass is "complete" if all sections of the text have some summary written for them. After this point, it'a important to step away from the annotation and return to it once the fine details of the text have left your working memory. 

An excellent annotation does two things: communicate the gist of each main point of a text, and easily allow a user/reader to delve deeper into the fine details of a point as curiosity demands. An excellent way to measure whether an annotation does this successfully is to *come back to it* after an extended period of time and re-read it. Then, ask yourself if you think you re-learned the text sufficiently. If not, that signals that the annotation needs a deep second pass. 

A second pass to clean up the annotation improves its structure towards this end with the following techniques especially helpful: 

### Separation of Concerns
Separation of concerns is a standard practice in software engineering where every minimal functional element in a program is identified and separated from the rest of the program. The same applies to any text: points can be separated into sections or notes, easing their future lookup. 

#### Separation into notes 
Scan through your annotation. If a heading can be the title of a note and the heading has more than a couple of sentences describing it, then spawn a new note and populate it with that title/content.

#### Separation of the Subjective and Objective 
It's always good to have opinions about a text and have those opinions out in the open in your annotation. But there is a difference between what a text says / intends to say and your evaluation of it. 

On a second pass, it's good hygiene to separate subjective appraisals of a point from the summary of the point itself. 

### Adding examples 
During my first pass, I typically sketch general points and principles from texts and internalize the examples and applications that I read. 

This is a great strategy for completing annotations in a timely manner. But again, the person reading an annotation (whether it be you from the future or anyone else) may not understand a principle in a text as quickly or have the extensive context and examples established in the text to reinforce that principle. 

To fix this, add [representative examples](<The Art of Constructing Representative Examples>) for each principle. 

>[!example] Example: Philosophies of Deep Work
Cal Newport in [*Deep Work*](<Annotation of Deep Work>) explains four philosophies of deep work: the monastic, bimodal, rhythmic, and journalistic. A representative example of a monastic is Donald Knuth; a bimodalist would be Carl Jung; a rhythmist would be Jerry Seinfeld; and a journalist would be Walter Isaacson.
>Each of these men organized their professional lives around their respective philosophy of deep work: Knuth spends many hours a day in dedicated study and is not easily reachable; Jung took long weekends in a cottage during his feud with Freud; Seinfeld regularly wrote jokes every day under a regimented routine to improve his comedy; Isaacson would work on his novels whenever he got the chance during the day, in the moment. 

### Summation 
Some texts have "summative structures" that serve as cumulative takeaways, summaries, references, or key points explicitly designed for future user reference. Take these and populate them in a note or section called "Summative Structures". 

After a second pass is done, the annotation is labelled “v2”.

# Third Pass
## Citation and Expansion
Almost all texts will have sources they derive from, and some of these sources are themselves worth delving into. If not, then they should at least be explicitly cited. 

Go through your annotation and look for any references to outside sources. Add a link or citation for every source. For any source that looks interesting enough to annotate, go ahead and spawn another annotation for that source and write the name/description of the text. 

You don't have to fill out the annotation at the same time you create it. You can always come back to it later. The important thing is that you plant the seed for the annotation and return to water it on a regular basis. 

After the third pass is done, the annotation is labelled “v3” and can be published. 

# Appendix 
## Annotation as [[Syngenesis]] as Guided Inquiry 
An annotation is an extension of the familiar idea of book reports, summaries or commentaries, but is implemented in a very specific and fast-paced way. 

First, annotations operate on the presumption that you have no memory. They assume, equivalently, that the book will disappear forever after you read it in this moment. So anything you want to internalize or save in your own notebook, you have to do it now. 

If I can convince myself that the above is the case, then the process of creating mental models and interpretations of what I’m reading happens *at the time I’m reading it*. 

An annotation is also an implementation of guided [self-directed inquiry](<Self-Directed Inquiry.md>), or self-directed inquiry that is concretely documented, systematized, and overseen in an orderly and deductive fashion. 

Intellectuals seem to have a deep sense for the right questions to ask, arguments and hypothesis worthwhile pursuing, and ways to phrase and write things robustly. This is likely built on a mountain of experience deductively working through ideas and arguments for years. For my sake, I want to document every single instance of me doing this for the rest of my life, in addition to meta-reflections and parallel reflections I have on my work *at the time that I do that work*. This is very challenging, but it’s the method that I’ve found actually helps me learn and retain knowledge for the long term. 

## Annotation as informal abstraction 
An annotation is *not* a copy of a text. It is *not* merely a descriptive summary of the text. It is *not* a commentary or opinion of a text. And it is *not* an original contribution of work. 

An annotation is an *informal abstraction* of a text: it explains the core ideas of the text in terms that someone familiar with its general background unfamiliar with its particulars can understand it after an initial reading. 

The goal of an annotation is twofold: (1) provide an uninitiated observer a quick view of my explanation of a set of ideas; (2) provide a conceptual map for me to refer back to when relearning or verifying an idea.
