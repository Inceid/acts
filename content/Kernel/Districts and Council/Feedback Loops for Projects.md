# The Problem
I like to come up with ideas for, start, and talk about projects much more than I like working on them consistently or finishing them. This is a big problem. I won't finish any of the projects I want to if I can't figure out a technique for finishing them while still enjoying or at least tolerating the process. 

The purpose of this strategy is to come up with a set of techniques to motivate myself to finish projects rather than start new ones. 

# Solutions: Leaning In
>Solutions that "lean in" seek ways to exploit my tendencies and energies rather than attenuate or contort them. 

## Reframing 
One of the best ways I've found to move past psychological blocks has been reframing negative ideas. For so long, I've thought of finishing projects as killing them or sending them into oblivion. 

The obvious inversion here is that finishing projects is just the opposite. It's a coronation event. It solidifies their legacy in your repertoire. Other than revision/refinement, it's just about the best thing you can do for a project. 

## Checkpoints
Instead of trying to “finish” a project, I can ask myself “what’s the next checkpoint?” and 
sprint from where I am to that checkpoint.

>*Example:* I had a lot of trouble motivating myself to finish `clox` towards the end of second year of medical school. But I was able to deal with this by chunking the project into discrete independent checkpoints, which the book helped out a lot with in how it was organized into “modular” chapters. So I went chapter by chapter, and when I didn’t have the time to finish a chapter in a single sitting, I went concept by concept per chapter. 

## Gradualism
Prior to establishing checkpoints and sprinting intensively towards them, it’s essential to break down a large project into gradually accelerated stages.

A **gradualist** approach to project management divides and schedules a large project this way.

## Tracking
Project progress should be explicitly and systematically tracked in some form that you find intuitive and appealing to repeatedly re-engage with. 

## Confidence: 'been here before'
In moments of frustration or setback, remembering that you’ve been in an analogous situation before and adopted some kind of practice to get through it. Everything that you’ve struggled with, you’ve found a way to surpass and make your own. Why would this situation be any different?

## Rewarding Yourself
Be careful here. 

You’re not stupid. If you reward yourself for finishing a project with a bunch of pizza, you’ll just say “I can just buy pizza without doing the work!” and stop working on projects. So you can’t just choose any arbitrary reward. 

The reward has to be tightly coupled to the project completion itself. There are some simple ways to do this:

1. `utility` The project itself is useful and can either be extended or applied to any other situation I’m in. If it’s useful or fun for other people to use, even better.
	- The corresponding reward is to *apply the project* or *use it as a service*.
2. `self-knowledge` The project tells you something important about yourself or your mind; or perhaps by completing it, you create a new way of existing and thinking.
	 - The corresponding reward is to *reflect on and mentally model the project*.
3. `presentation` Most people seem pretty confused but interested in the things that I’m working on. Presenting to an audience that appreciates and constructively criticizes my work on a regular basis motivates me to finish and expand it. 
	- The corresponding reward is to *present and talk about the project*.

# Effective Inversions for Negative Emotions
### From Passive to Active
I don’t want to sit around and spend most of my day memorizing medical facts. Thankfully, I don’t have to. I learn best by creating, applying, and reinforcing. 

So instead of spending all my time doing Anki, I’ll [build a medical knowledge base](<Building a Medical Knowledgebase>) of my own, ruthlessly edit and refine it, and apply it to my daily life. 

This is an instance of creating an active relationship with medical knowledge. Instead of just reading and flashcarding it, I’ll *apply* it to create things at a rapid pace.

I often feel like I have to force my mind to focus on something, whether it be a question/test, a lecture, someone talking to me while something else is on my mind, or anything else that I otherwise don’t immediately feel like thinking about or doing.

### From Frustration to Curiosity
I get frustrated with things easily; I get especially frustrated when systems that I expect to work don’t work. 

But I recognize frustration is counterproductive. Instead of stewing in the frustration, I can instead ask from a more neutral standpoint, “Why is this so frustrating? Is there some information I’m missing here?” and be genuinely curious about the missing details for solving a problem or advancing a project.
