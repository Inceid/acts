# Preamble 
The most important possible skill in the world we live in today is to be able to learn things quickly and deeply.

>How do I learn?

# Definition 
**Learning** is the act of acquiring new knowledge, values, or skills.

# Emulation and Creation
>I learn by [emulating](<Emulation.md>) the content I learn from. 

Emulation may occur in multiple forms, but typically involves creation:

1. Explicit paraphrasing or [re-creation](<Re-creation.md>) of the content in your mind’s “native" forms, or the forms it most easily understands and manipulates. 
	- This can start with simply copying or paraphrasing an idea in your own words. After that, you can start to use more creative or personalized encodings. 
	
2. Essaywriting: these can be [annotations](Annotation) or critical commentaries/appraisals with your own takes.

Emulation benefits from being supported by [encoding](<Encoding>) or [consolidation](<Consolidation>), which either takes the product of emulation and reduces it to its essential structure, or takes the details that separate different instantiations of a concept away, unifying them into a similar schematic model. 

# Operationalization
To learn, I must regularly *interact* with and *use* the content I learn. 

My brain is smart. It won’t remember unimportant things. And the things that it thinks are unimportant are the things it doesn’t use. So whatever I learn, I need to play with it or use it.

# Reinforcement
To retain what you learn, reinforce it on a regular basis. Methods of doing so are enumerate as follows:
* `Retrieval practice:` take practice tests, reconstruct from memory
* `Elaborative interrogation:` ask "how" and "why" questions, and elaborate on those questions towards more advanced mechanistic/teleological questions. Adapted from Socratic method.  
* `Backlinking:` link what you've learned to what you already know, either through: 
	* analogy,
	* explicit logical connection,
	* isomorphism or formal bijection.
-  `Distal application:` explicitly link what you've learned to something apparently unrelated. This may be operationalized through
	 - `stochastic:` inclusion of learned content in commonplace books (*Selfstudy*) and Second Brain platforms (`RI`)
	- the reason this works so well is that it enables this content to freely collide and bond with other content that has previously embedded itself within your mindscape. On the chance that some collisions create ideacules, those ideacules will stay in your head even longer, since strong associations and connections have long half lives in your mind.
	- `neostructural:` intentionally crafting distal analogies/applications. 
	- `paleostructural:` referencing previous integrative or summative designs (e.g. Cybernetics books for PhysioComputer SciencePhilosophy)

 To the point of memory models: We might say there are two kinds of reinforcement models for memory capture. 
 
1. The first is a congruent model, where the elements of the encoding of the model are elements of the actual thing being memorized. In this case, the thing being memorized is by itself "easy to remember". 
	- For me, these are elegant biochemical pathways, tightly coordinated regulatory paradigms, and ideas that are controversial, philosophically interesting, or challenge my beliefs about other things in some way. 
	- Another example is a biological mechanism that is relatively conserved or intuitive. 

2. The second is an imposed model, which is an encoding imposed on something difficult to memorize to make it easier. 
	- An example (specifically for studying medicine) is any Sketchy video. 

# Metamodels
>A **metamodel** is a higher-order conceptual model applied as a common interpretive scheme for multiple (potentially diverse) component models. 

Metamodels - also called [higher-order abstractions](<Higher-order abstractions>) - are flexible thought structures that enable fast, fluid multidisciplinary application. These function as advanced organizers for large volumes of heterogeneous concepts that must somehow be fit together into a cohesive framework.

# Dynamic Techniques 
Dynamic techniques are concerned with the modification of prior instantiated models, and their adaptation to new information in time.

# Appendix: Traditional and Contemporary Learning
Traditional Learning via lectures, textbooks, and assignments typically operates by feeding students large boluses of information that they are expected to rapidly digest on their own time and apply altogether to either solve artificially constructed problems or reconstruct artificially designed projects. 

Biology lab classes give their students complicated procedures for experiments that aren’t actually run by any biologists today to replicate for the sake of easy/standardized grading. Computer science classes assign labs to students that usually don’t directly correspond to any software they would implement in their careers (unless perhaps they were going into academia). And let’s not even talk about exams and whether they’re useful for anything.

In general, this model doesn’t work. Contemporary learning requires a learning methodology that:
1) Occurs *in medias res*, in the middle of things, while you’re doing everything else. You cannot pause your life to take a bunch of classes every time you need to learn a lot of stuff. You have to learn quickly *and* live your life.

2) Uses multiple sources: in medicine, technology, policy, and other rapidly evolving fields, there’s no single book to consult to stay up to date. You have to use multiple trusted sources and weigh them against one another in terms of their reliability. 

3) Rapidly applies knowledge: you can’t read a book, think about it for a bit, and then say you understand it. If you know something, you ought to be able to demonstrate and apply it. The gold standard for applying knowledge is to directly invoke it in a solution to a problem. So do that every chance you get.
	*You don’t know something if you don’t use it!*
