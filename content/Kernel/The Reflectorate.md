# Definition
**The Reflectorate** or **The Grid** is a network of physical notebooks that I use to coordinate my thinking and reflection in daily life.

# Motivation
>Why not just put all your thoughts into Obsidian?

Having separate non-redundant physical/analog and digital reflective systems is inspired by a few sneaking suspicions I have:
1. that manual writing and reflection from writing on paper activates different areas of our mind/brain than digital writing and reflection from writing on a screen; 
2. that physical/analog reflection and digital reflection can coordinate in dialogue to develop the mind far more effectively than either alone;
3. that it is possible to elegantly coordinate analog and digital reflective systems without excessive redundancy or cognitive overload.

In a world saturated with technological distractions, the prospect of navigating the dialectic between is also just intrinsically *interesting*.

So over the past year or so, I’ve decided to try to experiment with a dual reflective system featuring a set of connected physical notebooks juxtaposed by [*Republic Internum*](<What is Republic Internum>), my main digital reflective system and journal.

# Implementation

The Reflectorate consists of the following notebook groups.

## Selfstudy :: Undirected
*Selfstudy :: Undirected* or *SS* is a daily reflective journal that I use to take notes on my ideas and experiences whenever I feel like writing them down physically.

Typically, I write reflections in *SS* when I think putting them on paper and manually writing each letter will help me reflect on them more deliberately. Difficult emotions, problems, tasks and to-dos, and unstructured fleeting ideas are all recorded here. 

Especially unstructured/fleeting ideas might be written down in colored post-it notes. I try to gather these notes on a regular basis and stick them into *SS* on a page near the date that I recall or recorded writing the post-it note. 

All prior versions of *SS* are archived and physically stored nearby. 

## Selfstudy :: Directed
*Selfstudy :: Directed* or *dir* is a perennial reflective journal that I use to meditate intensively on and develop specific concepts that I’ve been mulling over for a longer period of time on paper. 

## Preclinical / Clerkship Notebooks
Preclinical and Clerkship Notebooks have all of my notes related to preclinical courses and clerkship experiences while I’ve been at Tufts for medical school. 

These are abbreviated as follows:
- $\text{MED}$ for Internal Medicine, my first core clerkship;
- $\text{OBGYN}$ for Obstetrics and Gynecology, my second core clerkship;
- $\text{FM}$ for Family Medicine, my third core clerkship;
- $\text{PED}$ for Pediatrics, my fourth core clerkship;
- $\text{PSY}$ for Psychiatry, my fifth core clerkship;
- $\text{SURG}$ for Surgery, my sixth core clerkship;
- $\text{NEURO}$ for Neurology, my seventh and final core clerkship.

## Philosophy Notebooks
I have two notebooks focused on my readings in Philosophy. 

The first, *Philosophy*, contains any descriptive/interpretive running analog notes on books/essays I’m reading.

The second, *Criticisms*, contains any commentaries of those works.

## Computer Science and Discrete Math
Since graduating Carnegie Mellon, I’ve maintained an interest in some of the deeper aspects of CS Theory and Discrete Math. I also wasn’t that great at either while in college. Given that and how fundamental and interesting both fields are, I have a perpetual desire to improve my understanding and problem-solving abilities in them. 

Figure 1 depicts the three main notebooks in this series: 
- *Linear Algebra*, which contains my notes and commentary of *Linear Algebra Done Right* by Sheldon Axler and *Linear Algebra Done Wrong* by Sergei Treil. I finished both books a while ago, but the notebook is an amazing prototype for how I want to develop future notebooks on dense mathematics and computer science topics. 
- *Proofs* contains my fully written-out proofs for specific central problems from different books, courses, or fields. 
- *PFPL* or Practical Foundations for Programming Languages contains my running annotative notes on the eponymously named book. I’ve been interested in both natural and programming languages for several years and have tried to carve out time to study both of them throughout my gap years and medical school.
