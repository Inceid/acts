# Definition 
**Self-as-system** is a paradigm of systems thinking that models oneself and self-identity as a system of multiple related parts. 

# Implementation 
## Implementation in isolation
Assume you are the only system that exists. How do you examine, design, and take control over it? 

### Examine your feedback loops
All of us are privy to certain inputs, events, and outputs that reinforce themselves. Eating a chicken nuggie makes me want more nuggies, until I feel sick and want exactly zero nuggies. An investment that yields a profit might make me want to put more into the investment, to yield more profit. When I watch Black Mirror, I get an urge to binge watch the rest of it. And many more examples.

The point is that, until we reach a certain physiologic “capacity” for enjoying or re-engaging in these activities, we will keep doing them. Each of these is a **feedback loop** in the system-that-is-us.

## Implementing Self-as-System in a world full of systems 
The world is saturated with systems that operate accordingly to their own rules and not necessarily to our preferred rules. 

Self-as-system implemented in our world requires the following: parallel development and emulation-recreation-assimilation.

### Parallel Development 
If you wish to develop yourself-as-system, you must do so in parallel to the systems around you. Otherwise, in the heat of development, you will more than likely lose

### Emulation-Recreation-Assimilation 
Emulation is the act of copying the behavior of a system. Recreation is the act of recreating a local model of the system within oneself that runs the minimal, essential instructions of the system and no more. Assimilation is the act of adopting those practices of exogenous systems that agree with one's local model as laws for the development of the local model. 

All of these must occur in a choreographed manner. 

## Eliminating the idea of Ownership 
Adoption of self-as-system-within-system blurs the boundaries between “self” as individually conceived and other such “selves”. That blurring allows you to effectively eliminate the idea of ownership as an incentive for action. 

>*Example:* If “I” own a house, but have adopted a definition of “I” that includes other bodies and animals who do not reside in that house or never will, is the house really mine? 

>*Example:* If “I” write and publish a book in “my” name, but have shifted the definition of “I” to encapsulate more minds than the one contained in this body, who does this book belong to? 

Intuitively, the answer is that everything belongs to every “self” or system that can agree to abide by the rules of engagement with the resource. Any system that would destroy the house upon interacting with it probably shouldn’t be able to reside in it.

All this aside, the point is that adopting self-as-system necessitates a paradigm shift as to one’s individualistic attitude towards ownership. We do not own the things we think we own. And, although we may not access certain objects nearly as much, since our identities as human beings are far more tied together than our individual minds may think, we own much more than we think we do on a daily basis.

### Eliminating Ownership speeds up Knowledge Management
Eliminating ownership makes it much easier for me to want to study medicine and to find efficient ways to study it. Previously, I had a lot of trouble wanting to study for Step 1 and wanting to study medical knowledge overall because I wasn’t able to cultivate a personal relationship with it. I was instructed to memorize so many things so fast, I didn’t have time to sit down and appreciate any of them, understand the history behind how we got to know so many of these things, or take some time to reflect on what kinds of medical knowledge I did like to study. 

# Advantages
## Depersonalization | [Otherization](<Self-as-Other.md>)
One of the major advantages of adopting a self-as-system mindset is that it gives you a break from individual responsibility. 

So many times and through so much subliminal cultural messaging, we’re taught that we are individuals who are fully responsible for our decisions and fully to blame for our failures. A simple application of systems thinking at a sociopolitical level disposes of this quickly. But beneath this myth lies another: that we are individual persons in the first place. 

Suppose you do believe you are an individual. Fair enough. But there is a beautiful release of tension that occurs when you adopt, at least for sake of suspension of disbelief, that you are one with every other mind and the universe, not distinct, that you are other.
